Understanding Ketamine’s Mechanism of Action

Ketamine’s anticonvulsant potential lies in its interaction with the brain. Seizures occur when electrical activity in the brain becomes intense and/or unbalanced, leading to disruptions in normal function. Ketamine works by targeting certain brain receptors, in particular NMDA receptors, that contribute to triggering seizures.

NMDA receptors help regulate electrical signals in the brain. When these receptors are overactive, they can contribute to the erratic brain activity seen in seizures. Ketamine blocks these receptors, helping to stabilize brain activity and reduce excessive electrical signals. By regulating NMDA receptors and controlling brain activity, ketamine offers powerful potential to reduce both the frequency and intensity of seizures.

The mechanism of ketamine differentiates ketamine from traditional therapies, which boost brain-inhibiting chemicals like GABA.

Ketamine targets the specific receptors that are involved in seizures, which makes this medication effective for cases that don’t respond to conventional treatments.

In these cases, ketamine has been used as a last-resort therapy. Protocols for ketamine use involve close monitoring by medical professionals to ensure its safety. Typically, ketamine is introduced only after other anticonvulsant medications have proven ineffective, making it a critical last-resort treatment in emergencies.

How is Ketamine Administered?

Ketamine can be administered through various routes, each with unique characteristics regarding onset, duration, and absorption. Each of the main methods — intravenous (IV), intranasal, oral, sublingual (including troches), and intramuscular (IM) — has different dosage standards:

Intravenous (IV) route: Ketamine takes effect quickly when administered directly intravenously into the bloodstream, since this treatment bypasses the digestive system. IV therapy also enables precise dosing, giving clinicians the most control over the treatment.

Intranasal route: Intranasal ketamine offers a relatively quick onset, albeit slower than IV infusions. This option may be ideal for those with an aversion to needles, who want a treatment that takes less time than an IV infusion, or prefer a non-invasive therapy.

Oral route: Oral ketamine must first be metabolized by the digestive system, leading to a lower overall absorption rate and slower onset of action. However, this option is highly convenient for ongoing home management of chronic conditions like pain or mood disorders.

Sublingual (including troches) route: Sublingual administration utilizes the membranes under the tongue for absorption, bypassing some of the digestive process. Troches (lozenges) slowly dissolve and are often used as part of ketamine-assisted psychotherapy programs.

Intramuscular (IM) route: IM injections may be used in acute emergency settings or situations lacking easy access to the patient’s veins (for IV infusions). IM injections also offer a relatively fast onset of effects.

The duration of a single treatment’s effects can vary, but may last between several days to a few weeks. In general, the most effective results come from a series of ketamine treatments, since the benefits build over time.

A healthcare provider will recommend a schedule of follow-up sessions to maintain symptom relief, based on how a patient responds to treatment.

Factors Influencing Dosage

Ketamine dosage varies widely, ranging between a microdose at or below 0.1 mg/kg, to a sub-anaesthetic dose of 0.5 mg/kg. Low doses may be beneficial for those who are sensitive to the dissociative or psychedelic effects of ketamine, while higher doses may be necessary if the patient has exhibited partial or no response, or if a faster onset of effects is needed.

High-dose ketamine sessions generally require professional supervision. A healthcare provider will monitor the patient’s heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration rate throughout the treatment, and help manage possible hallucinations or other adverse effects.
