JONATHAN NEMETH, 23 of Batavia, Illinois, used to wake up some mornings with intense muscle aches. At night, he was having seizures so violent that on two separate occasions, they fractured his spine. Nemeth, one of the nearly 3 million American adults with epilepsy, has tried more than a dozen anti-seizure medications. None stopped his convulsions. A year ago, he couldn’t work full-time. He has never been able to drive. He spent most days stuck at home, where he lives with his mom. He felt like a burden.

Electroencephalograms he had done at the University of Chicago revealed that his seizures stemmed from both sides of his brain, limiting his treatment options. When only one side is affected, doctors can remove bits of brain tissue on that side. “If you do it on both sides, patients lose their memory,” says Peter Warnke, MD, a professor of neurological surgery at UChicago Medicine.

A new option is in clinical trials, and Nemeth was still in the hospital after his second spinal fracture when Dr. Warnke told him about it. A stem cell treatment might target the hyperactive neurons, or brain cells, that cause the seizures. A neurotransmitter called gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) can reduce the firing rate of these neurons and stop the seizures, Dr. Warnke explains. So scientists genetically modify human stem cells into GABA-producing brain cells, then doctors place the cells in the brain’s temporal lobe, where seizures originate.

“This is the first treatment that addresses the biology of epilepsy, the hyperactive firing neurons, and that is fascinating,” says Dr. Warnke. The process has been 30 years in the making. “These are things scientists have dreamed of.”

In a three-hour surgery, Dr. Warnke drilled two small holes in the back of Nemeth’s head. Next, guided by robots, he threaded two tiny tubes into the patient’s brain to precisely deposit a liquid containing the stem cells.

Nemeth has been seizure-free since the procedure last February. “I don’t have to go to sleep in fear,” he says. He recently added more hours to his schedule as a cashier, and once he gets his driver’s license, he’ll apply for jobs that aren’t just within biking distance of his home. “This changed a lot of things, more than you might think,” he says. “It didn’t just help physically, but mentally as well.”

UP NEXT

With more research, this procedure could help people with other seizure disorders. It may also help with conditions such as schizophrenia or chronic pain that are marked by low levels of GABA in key brain regions. And scientists are developing stem cell treatments to boost dopamine in the brain, which could improve Parkinson’s disease symptoms.
