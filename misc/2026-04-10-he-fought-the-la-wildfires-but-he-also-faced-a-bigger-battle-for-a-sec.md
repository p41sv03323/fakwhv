# He Fought the LA Wildfires. But He Also Faced a Bigger Battle: For a Second Chance at Life.

A LINE OF 17 firefighters clambered up a smoky mountain as a Los Angeles neighborhood burned below them. In the span of 48 hours, the area had become a graveyard of cars left behind in flaming driveways. Living rooms glowing the color of lava. Palm trees turned into torches. Roofs disintegrating like pieces of fiery paper. It was January 9, 2025, and 34,000 acres of Los Angeles County had already been swallowed by the two blazes, fueled by the powerful Santa Ana winds.

“Damn,” Fausto Lopez said, feeling mesmerized and awestruck. This is really happening right now. Six feet tall and athletic, with long, messy black hair, Fausto wore a tangerine-colored uniform and helmet and carried a 50-pound pack on his back. He had been in the trenches of dozens of California wildfires over the past seven months. But not like this. Home after home obliterated. Neighborhood blocks leveled, as if someone had detonated a bomb. Fausto saw evacuees evading road barriers, dodging power lines, and clawing their way back into burn zones to find out what was left of their homes. He watched residents crumple in front of the ruins of their former lives, homes still smoldering or in flames. House after house, only brick chimneys still stood amid piles of burnt ash, twisted metal, melted glass.

Fausto’s fire crew had a mission: save the residences that had not yet been destroyed by the Eaton Fire in Altadena, California. The men stopped at the edge of a canyon, looking out over the black and orange terrain. Only a ravine separated them from the incinerating city now. Two miles away, flames encroached upon the land, thick with trees: black walnuts, red willows, live oaks, desert palms. Fresh kindling. Fausto understood the assignment. He excelled at it. Hike. Cut. Hydrate. Sleep. Survive. Contain the flames.

Fausto gripped his collapsible fire rake. He also had a rhino tool, a shovel blade that weighed as much as a brick, slung over his back. It was longer than his own legs. On the mountain, his crew established a fire line, which meant clearing brush, vegetation, trees, and weeds to create a kind of force field between the fire and the neighborhoods still standing. It was a full-body strength workout that could last hours, on and off, over a 24-hour shift. Fausto heaved, sweating as he hurled his body weight behind his blade.

By the third day of the wildfires, they would officially become the most destructive in L.A. County history, decimating 12,000 homes and buildings across Altadena and Pacific Palisades and ultimately killing 30 people. Eighteen of those deaths occurred in the Eaton Fire, which Fausto now faced. The winds had calmed a bit from the 80-mile-per-hour gusts of the days before. But he knew at any moment they could pick up or switch directions, sending embers shooting toward him like red-hot bullets.

At 20 years old, Fausto didn’t know what it felt like to lose a house in a fire. But he knew what it felt like to lose a future in a day. To wake up without the comforts that a home provides. To feel as if there is nothing left and suddenly find yourself stuck wondering who the hell you are. On the horizon, black smoke curled closer. It was hard to believe how the scenery of his life had metamorphosed in the span of four years—from inside his mother’s home to behind walls and into the woods.

Fausto was serving a seven-year sentence in the California penal system for a felony. Fighting this fire was his path to freedom. He felt time racing as the wind shoved at his back, the gusts growing in force. The fire was zero percent contained. And if Fausto wanted a chance to get out of the woods and beyond the walls that held him and back to his family, he needed to survive this first.

Alan M. Crogan Youth Treatment and Education Center, One Year Earlier

A FIGHT BROKE out inside the detention center for young inmates as Fausto sat at a computer, trying to finish a class online. Guards ordered everyone into a lockdown in their cells for hours until the situation was under control. Fausto, who had been serving time since age 17, knew going back to his cell would mean delays in completing his classwork online and he would have to play catch-up.

Before the historic wildfires devastated the Los Angeles area, and before Fausto became an incarcerated firefighter, he was arrested in 2021 at the age of 16 and sentenced to seven years in juvenile detention for his role in a shoot-out between two rival groups. “I shot at them first,” he says. “I didn’t kill nobody.” But the rivals shot back, killing the driver of the car Fausto was riding in. Their car then spiraled out of control, hitting and killing a bystander.

The police arrested him, and he was found guilty of second-degree murder. Fausto would not be released until age 24. In its case, the prosecution traced the entire crime to him as the main culprit. To Fausto, it didn’t feel fair, but he holds himself accountable: “I was there,” he says. “My actions did lead to it.” And he can’t erase the horrible reality: Two people died. “It’s something that I can’t unlive and can’t forget,” he says.

In lockup, alone with his thoughts, he ruminated. Why did I make bad choices? His father had tried to warn him to stay away from the “fast life,” which came with guns, money, girls, and nice cars. But Fausto didn’t listen. He had been hypnotized by it. “I was out there running amok,” he says. “My dad would lecture me and tell me there [would be consequences], but I was too impulsive, too immature, and too insecure to listen.” It wasn’t until he saw the repercussions of his actions that he finally understood what his dad was telling him.

Now, in the eyes of others, could he ever escape being branded a “bad guy”? Fausto didn’t know. But he knew in his heart that he was not evil. He knew his four sisters could see that in him. His parents too. Fausto told himself he could try to live a more noble and responsible life.

In detention, Fausto had to grow up fast. His father passed away not long after he was arrested, and Fausto was permitted to attend his funeral, but he could not be at home to help his mom and sisters through their grief. He looked around at his mostly bare single cell and told himself: I’m doing what I have to do to get out as soon as I can. Inside, everyone else became just “people you’re doing time with,” he says. “Nobody’s really your friend.”

After 15 months in juvenile hall in Indio, California, the city he was raised in, Fausto was moved to the Alan M. Crogan Youth Treatment and Education Center in Riverside, where he continued to keep his head down and avoid trouble. He focused on getting an education instead. After earning a high school diploma, he enrolled in a newly introduced college degree program for incarcerated scholars through Riverside City College.

“He learned to breathe deeply through the pain. Who are you? he asked himself along the way. One foot in front of the other. It felt as if he were walking on hot rocks.”

Carlos Cervantes, a formerly incarcerated mentor and teacher at the detention center, taught a course over Zoom to provide young inmates with the skills to transition into a college program. Cervantes met Fausto in the program in 2023 and remembers feeling struck by his commitment to an education, even when disputes among other inmates interrupted his schoolwork. Fausto was “so eager to learn,” Cervantes recalls. “He had the drive. The mindset.”

Fausto loved the feeling of dribbling a basketball, gliding down the juvenile hall court, and shooting free throws. Yet he would even skip his yard hours, which allowed him time to play hoops, in order to attend class and study. “Fausto always showed up,” says Michael Griggs, a program coordinator at Project Rebound Cal State San Bernardino, which supports formerly incarcerated students on the college’s campus. Griggs taught classes through Riverside City College at the facility where Fausto was detained. He remembers how Fausto “was always engaging. He asked a lot of good questions.”

Fausto kept his cell tidy, organizing his deodorant, toothpaste, shampoo, conditioner, and “smell-good lotion,” as the inmates called it (Bath & Body Works scents like Ocean and Graphite that his sisters gave him). He also kept a religious cross, which he made by ironing beads together, and a Bible, which he had only recently started reading, taking inspiration from Ephesians 6:11: “Put on the whole armor of God so that you may be able to stand firm against the strategies of the devil.”

After graduating from the Riverside City College program in June 2024, Fausto hoped to be admitted into a work-release program that was in development at the detention center. It would allow him to hold a regular job in the community and return to the facility after clocking out. Fausto wanted to earn decent wages, not the less-than-74-cents-an-hour pay many inmates earned working in prisons. He also wanted to build his résumé, so he would be ready to work and attend college upon his release. He hoped to study science and medicine, try out for the basketball team, and eventually provide health services in the nearby low-income city he grew up in.

But right before Fausto graduated, becoming the first student at his detention center to earn an associate’s degree, the staff let him know he had a different assignment to complete. In a week, he would be shipped off to Pine Grove Youth Conservation Camp to train to be a firefighter in Northern California, 442 miles away.

The California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation started the fire camp program in 1915, expanding it during World War II after men who had worked in the forestry services left to serve in the war. Inmates took their place, and more fire camps were established. Today, there are 35 camps housing 1,800 inmates across the state. To qualify, they must have eight years or less remaining on their sentence and a low-security classification based on good behavior. Legislation passed in 2020 made it easier for fire crew members to have their records expunged and obtain firefighting jobs upon release.

Incarcerated firefighters fill gaps in personnel during wildfire season at a time when the work is becoming more treacherous and demanding due to climate change. Supporters say the fire camp program provides a job path for inmates. The Anti-Recidivism Coalition—a nonprofit organization that works to end mass incarceration in California—also recently helped develop an 18-month firefighting training and reentry program for those released from the prison system. But some residents and labor activists contend the fire camp program is a form of indentured servitude, with inmates risking their lives for low pay (incarcerated firefighters receive between $5.80 and $10.24 a day, with an additional $1 per hour during active emergencies) under dangerous conditions.

Fausto remembers feeling caught off guard by the assignment. They told him without warning, Okay, you’re gone. Get up out of here. He could have said no, but he did not object. He figured at least fire camp would be an opportunity to earn “time credits,” receiving one day off his sentence for every day he served on a fire crew. Days were already being knocked off his sentence as his accomplishments, academic milestones, and marks for good behavior added up, but he still had four years left to go.

Fausto had yet to wrap his mind around it all when he found himself on a flight to Sacramento, accompanied by two probation officers. It was his first plane ride. His first time ever leaving Southern California. He had a window seat, and he looked out over the landscape, marveling at the greenery as they approached Northern California.

A fire camp employee picked him up in a van at the airport. From there, they drove an hour and a half to Pine Grove. Deeper into the woods than Fausto had ever been.

Pine Grove Youth Conservation Camp

THE FIRE CAMP sits on forest land 65 miles southeast of Sacramento, in Amador County, in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada Mountains. Pine Grove is known for its abundance of trees: white fir, sugar pine, white bark pine, ponderosa pine, gray pine, lodgepole pine. The prickly needles and spirally cones cluster on the ground like fallen ornaments. The inmates at the Pine Grove fire camp are not fenced in. They sleep in a dorm, not cells. To Fausto, it felt freer—and secluded from society in a good way, he says. “It’s beautiful.”

In early July, on the day Fausto arrived, he met six men housed at the camp. The other dozen in residence were away fighting a fire, so the camp seemed sprawling, empty, and quiet. But the tempo quickly picked up. More inmates were set to arrive soon, just as fire season was exploding across the state, with temperatures soaring. It would become the hottest month on record: reaching 113 degrees in Sacramento and 119 degrees in nearby Redding. Other areas would set records for consecutive days over 100 degrees.

Fausto felt exhilarated by the environment. It was a new start. A fresh chance to redeem himself under the legal system. He wanted to take full advantage and became driven to learn everything he could. He spent over a week in a classroom learning about the science and behavior of fire from the camp staff. He learned how to use a chainsaw and the other tools—the rhino, the pulaski, and the mcleod, which has a blade and rake-like teeth.

Training involved two “hell weeks.” It was a crash course in physical and mental preparation to join the fire crew. Six days a week, Fausto hiked and ran through the woods. The goal: to complete four miles in less than an hour and 15 minutes, wearing all of his fire gear.

“Ever since he was locked up, he’d turned the question on himself: Who are you? How did a man like him answer this kind of question? Fausto knew he was a son. A sibling. A kid who had made bad mistakes, only to one day wake up as a man who had missed out on being a kid.”

The trainees wore uniforms and heavy, eight-inch-high, fire-resistant boots all day—a requirement because when on call as firefighters, they would have under three minutes to deploy. Fausto normally wore size 11 shoes. But upon his arrival, the camp did not have his size. So he wore size 13 boots through his training and hikes.

As the crew got to know each other better, Fausto befriended an inmate, Gonzalo, who taught him how to sharpen the mcleod blade at a 45-degree angle. The man became his co-leader on the crew, and Fausto looked up to him.

Once, Fausto asked Gonzalo a question that had long stuck with him because he could not come up with an adequate answer himself: “Who are you?” Fausto asked. Not his name or age. But at his deepest core. Who are you?

“I’m a man of business,” Gonzalo told him.

That lodged in Fausto’s psyche. Ever since he was locked up, he’d turned the question on himself: Who are you? How did a man like him answer this kind of question? Fausto knew he was a son. A sibling. A kid who had made bad mistakes, only to one day wake up as a man who had missed out on being a kid.

During training, Fausto’s feet blistered so badly that they turned pink and raw. He learned to breathe deeply through the pain. Who are you? he asked himself along the way. One foot in front of the other. It felt as if he were walking on hot rocks. When he removed his clunky boots, it looked like he had second- or third-degree burns. One corrections officer saw his blisters and suggested he quit or at least be held back from finishing the fire crew training until he recovered.

“I don’t want to quit,” Fausto told the officer.

The officer talked to the fire captain, who said, “If he doesn’t want to quit, then you guys can’t make him.”

Fausto had proved to himself that he had the intellect and the work ethic to earn a college degree. He still desperately wanted to go to college full-time. But the blisters, the pain, the weeks of training hell—all of it showed him something else about himself: He was physically and mentally stronger than he realized.

By August, Fausto was a firefighter.

He fought blazes up and down the state. Most people have no idea how many fires are burning regularly—at least one a week. Fausto’s crew was often awakened in the middle of the night and sent to a fire. The crew members were required to drink a quart of water an hour daily. One day, Fausto failed to drink enough and ended up so dehydrated that he had to go to the emergency room, where they shot him up with liquid IVs and potassium.

The crew’s backpacks held five pounds of food—beef jerky and sandwiches with cheese that they would grill over the wildfire still ablaze right next to them. During the 24-hour shifts, Fausto slept on the charred ground on the side of a mountain, next to the burning fire. It can take weeks or even months to fully contain a wildfire, with fire crews rotating around the clock with little rest. “If you sleep in the green areas, there’s a chance the fire can burn through there while you’re sleeping,” Fausto says. “So, we sleep where it’s already burned.”

It could also get painfully cold at night. “You are working all day. Your body is drenched from your head to your knees, full of sweat. You don’t shower. You’re out there from 7 to 7, so when it’s time to sleep, you’re freezing,” Fausto says. “And the wind is just blowing because you’re at the top of the mountain.” They had no shelter, no blankets, only sweaters to cover them. Guards did not monitor them, only a fire chief from the camp, who was not a corrections officer.

The week before the Palisades and Eaton blazes erupted, Fausto and his crew had already been traveling to other assignments across the state. They had just returned to Pine Grove when they got the news from the camp staff about the Los Angeles fires: They’re just burning through everything, eating everything in their path. The National Weather Service office in Los Angeles had sent out a red flag warning with a “Particularly Dangerous Situation” alert, the highest possible level of fire risk.

It was a seven-hour drive from Pine Grove to Altadena. Fausto felt a rush of excitement. This fire, he knew, was a big one. And it was closer to home for him. He wanted to get there and do his job. He and other men loaded onto a bus: “We’re leaving, we’re leaving, we’re leaving!”

Altadena

FAUSTO'S CREW PULLED up under a dark and flaming-red sky in the late-night hours of January 8. To help the more than 12,000 firefighting and emergency personnel, over 1,000 incarcerated firefighters were deployed to fight the flames in the Palisades and Altadena. The smoke could gag you. It felt frigid outside with the fierce wind.

Fausto and his fellow firefighters slept in the bus, squeezing into every corner of the floor and every seat they could find. Fausto’s knees smashed against a seat back. His crew’s shift would begin in the morning. He slept for a few hours and awoke with body aches, then loaded his backpack with roughly 50 pounds of water, supplies, and gear before setting off toward the path of the fire. Over 50,000 residents in the vicinity of the Eaton Fire had been evacuated.

Fausto was one of two men leading the fire line. The first man in charge, his friend Gonzalo, carried a radio and a medical kit with supplies like tourniquets to control hemorrhages and gauze to treat wounds. Fausto also had a radio, as well as an automated external defibrillator to deliver electric shocks if any of his fellow firefighters went into cardiac arrest.

The inmate crews worked until their muscles ached and their feet throbbed. The local fire crews did, too, Fausto says. But he felt the inmates did the hardest work, as they cut the fire lines to aid the local crews, scraping the soil and creating a barrier to starve the fire of fuel to burn. “Envision a tunnel,” he says. “Straight clear through the brush.”

The men did it all with their bodies and tools alone—no trucks, no heavy machinery. Chopping, raking, carving, and scraping. Fausto had also learned how to identify different types of heat. Radiant heat—the kind that stings the skin like the hot sun. Convection heat—the kind that rises in the air, like inside an oven. Conduction heat—the kind that travels through direct contact with objects. He felt all these forms of heat in the fires, his face at times kissing the flames, which were close enough to lash at his cheeks.

According to preliminary findings from the L.A. Fire HEALTH Study, which aims to assess the health impact of the wildfires, the firefighters who fought the Palisades and Eaton blazes showed lead levels five times greater and mercury levels three times greater than a control group of Northern California forest firefighters. Lead and mercury exposures have been associated with neurological impairments, like loss of peripheral vision, speech problems, and muscle weakness.

Fausto didn’t care to think about how the fires would affect his health. He didn’t think about being a hero or saving lives. He wanted to prove his commitment to this job. Day after day, the routine repeated: 24-hour shifts with a couple hours of sleep on the mountain. By January 13, six days after the Eaton Fire started, it had burned more than 14,000 acres and was 33 percent contained. But weather officials warned that more dangerous winds—55 to 70 miles per hour, the kind that could kick up embers and start entirely new blazes—were on the way.

The Rose Bowl

DURING THEIR BRIEF REST PERIODS between 24-hour shifts, Fausto’s crew returned to the Pasadena Rose Bowl, at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains, to refuel and rest. Famous for its college football games and concerts, the stadium had been transformed into a staging ground for first responders, now lined with bright-red fire trucks and rows of hundreds of tents and trailers with bunks and showers.

The taqueros of Los Angeles came out in force, descending upon the base camp packed with nearly 4,000 firefighters, volunteers, and incarcerated workers. With taco carts and trucks equipped with smokestacks, they served tacos de birria de res, corn tortillas, consomé, café de olla, and carne asada. Another vendor brought two carts to make 500 hot dogs.

Cervantes, Fausto’s mentor and teacher, also showed up to the Rose Bowl. He happened to have a taco truck catering business on the side, and he came to feed the first responders—200 people on the first day, 170 the next. While at the Rose Bowl, he spotted his former student Fausto.

Cervantes contacted Griggs: Fausto is here, fighting the Eaton Fire. Coincidentally, Griggs needed to talk to Fausto, but there had been no way to get in touch with him for days.

For the past month, Griggs had been communicating with the probation office weekly about Fausto’s case. He knew Fausto had the potential to leave the system and follow a path to higher education and a career, perhaps even working toward a PhD, as he was. He also knew Fausto had been a model inmate. If anyone deserved an early release, Griggs knew Fausto did. “I ended up writing a reentry plan for him,” says Griggs, who earned a master’s degree specializing in forensic social work. He had sent all of Fausto’s files to probation. “I was on the phone with them three or four times a week, for hourlong calls, trying to plan all these things out, because we didn’t have a direct line to talk to Fausto.”

As the Eaton Fire burned, Griggs finally received a decision. The probation office told him: We want to support Fausto going to college. His tuition and part of his housing would be covered by a federal Pell Grant. The paperwork had been filed and approved. The plan just needed a final decision of support from the judge, and Fausto would be a free man.

Hoping to find Fausto, Griggs volunteered to serve tacos with Cervantes at the Rose Bowl. While at the base camp, he saw comedian Nick Cannon and California governor Gavin Newsom. But he did not see Fausto.

“He’s actually fighting a fire right now,” someone told Griggs. “But if you want to come back tomorrow, you’ll be able to see him.”

The next day, Griggs returned. This time, he found Fausto, who wore beige boots and an orange jumpsuit with the words cdcr prisoner on the back and running down his right leg. He had a mustache, and his long black hair looped through the back of a baseball cap with the words pine grove camp 12.

“Have you talked to probation?” Griggs asked.

“No,” he replied.

Probation, Griggs told him, had approved the plan. “You’re going to college.”

Fausto couldn’t believe it. His first thought was: Damn, I’m leaving and everybody else is staying. He thought of his friend Gonzalo, the co-leader, and felt a sense of guilt. But Fausto did not have time to dwell on it. He was soon headed to another 24-hour shift on the Altadena fire line. He wouldn’t be able to follow up with Griggs, or the judge, until he returned.

“It was crazy,” Cervantes remembers, “a person like Fausto, that’s getting ready to be released, still on the front lines, risking his life.” Fausto was so close to freedom. Still, Cervantes says, “you don’t know if he’s going to come home.”

The Mountaintop

NINE DAYS HAD passed since the Los Angeles blazes started. The Eaton Fire was now reaching 55 percent contained. On the mountain again that day, Fausto thought of Griggs. Could it be true? Could he actually be going to college? Cal State San Bernardino, the same campus where Griggs worked?

Fausto imagined his life as a college student. Life in the world he had been cut off from for the past four years. He enjoyed the camaraderie in fire camp. He appreciated his crew. But it was an opportunity to be free, three and a half years earlier than his original sentence had called for. It’s my time, he thought, to close this chapter in my life.

Fausto finally finished his 24-hour shift and returned to the base camp. On his 10th day at the fires, he was allowed to meet with the judge and a probation officer via phone. The judge approved his release—a probation officer was on the way to pick Fausto up from the Rose Bowl immediately. After seven months on the job, he would be dropped off at Cal State San Bernardino, 55 miles away.

Cal State San Bernardino

IN THE BACK of a classroom at Cal State San Bernardino on a February afternoon, Fausto sits at a desk, his hair freshly braided, wearing an Adidas jacket, jeans, and headphones. No one who saw him walking around campus, some days in his college hoodie and CSUSB Coyotes sweatpants, would ever know where he came from or what he had seen or done in the past year, the fires he’d faced.

The campus is home to over 85 formerly incarcerated students whom Griggs works with through Project Rebound, which helps integrate them into college. “There are students at the bachelor’s level, the master’s level, and we have alumni who are in doctoral programs, law programs, founders of nonprofits, clinician therapists,” he says. “There’s no telling what you can do.” Griggs adds that he is also an alumnus of Project Rebound and is now pursuing his PhD in social welfare at UCLA. “So, it’s all possible.”

On campus, Fausto has enrolled in yoga, human ecology, philosophy, the history of rock ’n’ roll, and understanding capitalism—credits that count toward his degree. He works out regularly at the gym and is hoping to try out for the basketball team. He is majoring in biology and plans to become a doctor.

“Fausto is exactly where he needs to be,” says Griggs. He will graduate with a bachelor’s degree with students his age. “It is very meaningful and emotional,” he adds, tearing up when talking about it. “A representation of what second chances look like. It could have turned out completely different. He could have gone into the adult system. He could have spent years there.”

Fausto thinks of his fire crewmate Gonzalo, who’s still locked up, still fighting fires. The one who told him, I’m a man of business.

“That has stuck with me,” Fausto says. “It is how I approach life.”

He thinks of the families of his victims and wants them to know he is not living without remorse. “I’m here and I’ve got to do things right and make amends for my family, myself, and the people and community that I’ve hurt.”

He recalls his days of studying through fights, hiking on blistered feet, sleeping on the sides of brutally cold mountains, the feeling of flames flickering at his cheeks, his father’s death. Never again will he go back to being locked up, he tells himself.

“Now,” Fausto says, “I’m trying to be who I am.”
