# The Doctor, the Biohacker, and the Quest to Treat Their Long COVID

THE DOCTOR IS HERE NOW. Matthew Light, MD, pops onscreen in his baby-blue T-shirt beneath a cardigan. He’s 42, with hair graying on the sides, a narrow nose, thick dark eyebrows. Patients from all walks of life log on from living rooms, kitchens, sofas, beds. Some are at home with their cameras off, while others rally a week’s worth of energy to be here in person on this frosty winter night, in a conference room at Colorado’s UCHealth Medical Center of the Rockies, attending this rare kind of support group led by Dr. Light.

There is an understanding among them, a camaraderie. They know Dr. Light is like them. And it is not easy right now to be them. He gets it. Everyone here gets it. What it’s like to be doubted by friends and loved ones. To have their symptoms disregarded by strangers or misdiagnosed by doctors. How brain fog can submerge the mind some days, thoughts sloshing around in a murky soup. He endures the fatigue, understands how it comes in spurts and hits like a tsunami in the early afternoon. It is a weighted sled he cannot stop pushing. Like all of them, he desperately wants to feel better.

Walking on a treadmill at home, Levi Henry listens in via Microsoft Teams through a pair of bulky headphones, his shaggy blond-brown curls tucked beneath an army-green beanie. Before he got sick, the 35-year-old had been in the best shape of his life, doing “Murph” CrossFit workouts—300 squats, 200 pushups, 100 pullups, followed by a one- or two-mile run—daily. But his breathing began to feel like a constant whacking to the throat, cutting off air with each inhale. The difficulty in catching his breath was like trying to run wearing an extremely restricting corset. This oxygen choke hold triggered anxiety, something he’d never really had before. He had to remind himself: You’re not gonna die right now.

Never one to believe that doctors have all the answers, Levi does not show up to this long COVID support group seeking specialists with expertise in his condition. He comes for the regular folks like him who are commiserating freely, troubleshooting potential treatments without feeling judged, discussing everything from digestive problems to nerve pain, sweating, numbness, vertigo, tingling, dizziness, difficulties smelling or tasting, trouble socializing, and sleeping. They turn to each other, because who better to turn to? Science does not yet have the answers.

Five years after the COVID-19 pandemic first swept across the planet, as many as 20 million Americans and at least 400 million people worldwide suffer from long COVID, a debilitating condition that still has no approved treatment. Some people find themselves unable to participate in normal activities, like working or socializing. Many are turning to remedies they hear about online—even if that means putting their bodies at risk—and poring over small studies that might show some evidence of symptom relief, in a desperate attempt to feel better again.

Not only is there no known cure, but there isn’t even a clear understanding of what it is. Researchers believe that long COVID—which, according to the American Academy of Neurology, is the third-leading neurological disorder in the U.S., behind tension-type headaches and migraines—likely has many subtypes that affect the brain, lungs, heart, kidneys, and digestive system. The disease may manifest differently and respond differently to treatments, making research challenging. Meanwhile, clinical trials have been slow to complete, as patients are often unable to participate because they are homebound, too unwell, or disabled. One novel phase 2 drug trial, launched in 2023, is allowing patients to participate from their own homes, but we won’t be able to fully grasp the long-term consequences of the condition for years to come.

All of this has led Levi and the others here to this very room. Levi hadn’t planned on meeting, much less liking, Dr. Light. If nothing else, this pulmonologist was a bit of a curiosity, he thought: A doctor who has long COVID too? But maybe this could be his chance to get a lead on a breakthrough treatment. To finally feel like himself again.

Levi

December 2022

LYING ON HIS BACK in a hospital gown, Levi felt himself sliding backward into a glass dome. It was a hyperbaric oxygen therapy chamber, which administers oxygen at air pressure levels up to three times higher than a person would breathe normally. As a result, more oxygen dissolves into the bloodstream.

The treatment, which can last up to two hours, is commonly used for speeding up muscle recovery, as well as treating decompression sickness in scuba divers, carbon monoxide poisoning, trapped air bubbles in blood vessels, and diabetic foot ulcers. But Levi was using it for a different reason. In his online research, he came across some hopeful studies about the treatment. A 2021 study of just 10 patients found reduced fatigue and better cognitive function after hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and a 2022 study of 73 people saw improved neurocognitive function and diminished symptoms post-COVID. So he decided to find a clinic and try it.

Levi lay in silence as his ears popped. He could talk to staff members through an intercom system. But if he felt claustrophobic or started to panic, it could take up to 20 minutes to exit the chamber, so as not to cause eardrum damage from a rapid change in pressure. He was warned, before entering, of other possible risks as well: seizures, temporary nearsightedness, oxygen poisoning, even fire. (Though the chambers are deemed generally safe, a 5-year-old boy was recently killed inside a hyperbaric chamber at a Michigan medical facility when it exploded during his treatment, and in another case a 37-year-old died after suffering an oxygen-toxicity seizure in one.)

No matter. This would be just one of many treatments Levi would try since he first contracted COVID in December 2021. He and his wife, along with their toddler and infant, came down with the virus after a family holiday party. Though a vaccine had been available for 12 months, Levi’s family had opted not to get it, unsure of the side effects it might have on their bodies, particularly that of their unborn child (his wife was pregnant with their second child when the vaccine first became available in December 2020). Taste and smell disappeared. Body aches set in. They spent Christmas Day exchanging gifts with other family members outside on their porch.

His BRAIN FOG felt like a LINGERING FUZZINESS that reminded him of what it was like to WEAR A CROOKED FOOTBALL HELMET. But this helmet he could NOT TAKE OFF.

Five days later, a ferocious wildfire, propelled by 100-mile-per-hour winds, licked at the edges of their suburban town of Westminster, Colorado, 16 miles north of Denver. Worried the inferno would charge toward them as they slept—and they wouldn’t even be able to sense it without their smell intact—Levi and his family evacuated. They checked into a hotel, weakly carrying the few bags of luggage they could muster the energy to pack, and collapsed on the bed, feeling defeated, thinking, This is the day the world is going to end.

But it didn’t. By New Year’s Eve, Levi and his family had returned to their house. His other family members got better, and Levi, who would soon return to working from home as an actuarial manager for a nonprofit health-care company, thought he had to be getting better too.

Except the symptoms worsened. His breathing suddenly felt labored. His brain fog felt like a lingering fuzziness that reminded him of what it was like to wear a crooked football helmet. But this helmet he could not take off. The fatigue hit at different moments. He tried to run, but his strength sputtered out abnormally fast and he had to nap for hours after, as if he had run a full marathon. He tried reading books to his kids at night but felt so wiped out. Doctors told him, “Well, you have young kids. Aren’t all parents wiped out?” But Levi knew that this kind of exhaustion was not just from parenting. Their advice was not useful. It actually felt like gaslighting. It did not take Levi long to realize on his own: This is long COVID. The term had only recently emerged into the public consciousness, created in the spring of 2020 by people who had not seemed to recover from the virus.

It would take the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine two more years to announce the definition of long COVID, a condition that still has no clear blood test or widely accepted biomarker. The definition says that symptoms persist at least three months after infection and may include difficulty breathing, brain fog, dizziness, extreme fatigue, loss of taste or smell, and fast heart rate. It can also lead to stroke, blood clots, and diseases like postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), fibromyalgia, and diabetes.

Levi’s original major as an undergrad at the University of Colorado Boulder was biology, on the pre-med track. He had always been fascinated by health but never thought traditional Western medicine had all the answers. “I tend to go more of the Eastern medicine approach…vegan, herbs, supplements, traditional Chinese medicine,” he says. A college advisor told Levi he might want to consider switching majors. He loved math and data and decided to major in that instead. As time went on, he continued to gravitate toward the idea that “you’re your own doctor.” Long COVID put this into even sharper perspective for him.

Levi saw many clinicians, including two nurse practitioners and a nutritionist, within the first two years of living with the condition. He told them his theory that he had long COVID, and based on his symptoms, they agreed with him. But no one had conclusive answers. Doctors and researchers seemed to be hitting walls as much as the public was in trying to understand specific causes and come up with surefire treatments. Meanwhile, long COVID patients and advocacy groups had long been pushing for a more rapid, urgent government response.

Unwilling to just sit around and wait for slow clinical trials and other underfunded scientific research to come through, Levi decided he would take up researching potential long COVID treatments himself. This approach lined up with his own health beliefs. “I’ve always told people, ‘If I get cancer, I’m not doing chemotherapy.’ And I stand by that,” he says. “I will do more natural treatments, anything under the sun.”

He immediately began scouring medical journals, news articles, forums, Reddit and Facebook communities, newsletters, and websites to formulate his own game plan, starting with hyperbaric oxygen therapy. In all, Levi would complete 20 sessions at the Rocky Mountain Hyperbaric Institute over the next year, and he felt like they helped with some of his breathing issues. But the treatments were expensive—about $200 each—and since this is an experimental therapy, insurance did not cover it. His search continued.

Dr. Light

February 2023

A COUPLE YEARS before the pandemic hit, Dr. Light finished his medical fellowship at the University of California San Diego. Both of his parents had worked in health care when he was growing up, and he ended up becoming a doctor—in pulmonary and critical care, treating lung disease at the UC San Diego hospital and the San Diego VA Medical Center. As COVID spread, all of a sudden patients everywhere needed him. It was exciting, terrifying, and dreadful.

In 2020, during the early days of the pandemic, he was working at a hospital one night and intubating a woman suffering from COVID. Usually when he inserted the breathing tube, the respiratory therapist would clamp it so the patient’s spit and other bodily secretions wouldn’t spew out everywhere. But this time, the therapist forgot, and Light was sprayed with all the fluid in her lungs.

Though he spent day after day around patients, his COVID tests always came back negative. As a frontline doctor, when the first vaccine was approved, he was among the first to receive it. At 39, he considered himself to be in good health at the time. He rode his Peloton bike for an hour a couple of times a week, at one point burning 1,100 calories in a single ride. Even if COVID did get to him, Dr. Light thought he stood a good chance of rebounding. “I was a little cavalier about it,” he says.

His wife is from Colorado, so in 2021 they decided to move to the Denver area with their two young kids to be near family. Dr. Light eventually landed a job at the UCHealth Pulmonary Clinic. That same year, he and his wife found a home in Westminster. It took him over an hour to get to the clinic, but the commute was worth it for the chance to live in a peaceful, family-oriented neighborhood, in a town known for its walking trails, farmers markets, and craft breweries.

At one point, Dr. Light asked himself, AM I THE PROBLEM? Did I just take care of a lot of COVID patients and see a lot of people die, and now that I have COVID, it just scares the crap out of me?

Still, the pandemic lingered, with an estimated 34.5 million COVID cases reported in 2021. Everything from antivirals like Paxlovid, remdesivir, and Lagevrio to more dubious drugs like hydroxychloroquine and ivermectin had been suggested as potential treatments for the virus. But none of these had shown any significant success in preventing long COVID, which more and more people began sharing their experiences with online.

In February 2021, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) announced what is now called RECOVER: Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery Initiative—a $1.15 billion nationwide research program established to understand, treat, and prevent long COVID. And it needed to happen quickly: By the end of the following year, an estimated 7 percent of U.S. adults reported having had long COVID, with those ages 35 to 49 the most likely to have had it in the past or to have it currently. The National Research Action Plan on Long COVID, created by the Department of Health and Human Services under the Biden administration, was also underway. But many wondered if all of this would be enough and if the research momentum would carry on, as pandemic fatigue had already taken over the nation.

On a February afternoon in 2023, Dr. Light was at the clinic when muscle aches took hold of him. It felt like the flu, but different, and he suspected what it was. He couldn’t dodge it forever, and he figured his time had finally come: Dr. Light tested positive for COVID. He stopped at Whole Foods on the way home and bought supplies to hunker down with in the basement and watch Netflix for a few days. A staycation. But two days into it, he felt violently short of breath, as if somebody had taken a belt and wrapped it around his chest, squeezing, refusing to let go.

He went to the emergency room and got chest X-rays. But they looked okay, and so did his oxygen level. Doctors would listen to his lungs and say, “Well, it doesn’t sound too bad.”

“As a pulmonologist, I don’t know what to tell you,” Dr. Light replied. “I really can’t breathe.”

They sent him home, but he returned to the ER two more times, with no clear test results or diagnosis. He tried to gulp for air but felt as if he were drowning instead. Eight weeks passed. I can’t work. I can’t take care of my kids. I can barely sit here on the couch without panicking. Dr. Light tried inhalers, pulmonary medication samples. He even did an ultrasound of his own heart.

To be a pulmonologist struggling to breathe felt like a special kind of hell. His mind raced with all the potential COVID-related problems: Inflammation of the heart. Constriction of the heart by the pericardium, the surrounding sac. Blood clots in the lungs. Organizing pneumonia, an inflammation that can damage the lungs but is not an infection like general pneumonia. Is that what’s happening to me? A chest X-ray, he knew, catches organizing pneumonia only half the time.

At one point, Dr. Light asked himself, Am I the problem? Did I just take care of a lot of COVID patients and see a lot of people die, and now that I have COVID, it just scares the crap out of me?

He barely managed day by day. By May, Dr. Light thought he felt somewhat better and would take a chance and visit a friend in Ojai, California. The travel and socializing demolished him. His shortness of breath returned with a vengeance. He felt demoralized and incapacitated.

Six months after testing positive for COVID, with no relief from his symptoms, Dr. Light thought, Could this be what people are referring to as long COVID? He reached out for specialized help. Access to long COVID specialists can be difficult, as there are so few out there, but he heard about a clinic specializing in long COVID at the University of Colorado, part of the same health system in which he works. Dr. Light sent an email to a doctor: “Can I come and talk to you about this? Because I just am not feeling better.”

The visit finally put all his symptoms into perspective. The specialist told him, “You have a typical long COVID symptom—shortness of breath with activity—and it sounds like you have developed chronic fatigue syndrome as a result of COVID.”

Dr. Light was surprised that it had taken someone this long to put the pieces together. Now he had a diagnosis. Long COVID. This is what I’m up against. Still, the core problem remained: Even specialists didn’t have a clear-cut treatment plan. None exists.

Medical care was Dr. Light’s profession. And this was his life. Now he just had to find a way to recover. And if he could do that, maybe he could help other patients who were feeling lost or on their own too.

His wife told him he Googled everything too often. But how could he not? The same questions that haunted him also befuddled doctors: What actually is causing long COVID? Today it’s understood that between 10 and 30 percent of people who contract COVID-19 will experience long COVID. There are a handful of main theories as to what causes it, but research suggests that a particular protein that forms a spike on the surface of the virus continues to circulate in some long COVID patients’ blood up to 14 months after infection. Other studies show that the COVID virus may trigger other autoimmune conditions or the chronic inflammation of cells.

The jury is still out on the role the COVID vaccine plays in all of this. In one study published in The Lancet Infectious Diseases, people who were unvaccinated at the time of a COVID infection were at greater risk of developing long COVID. A recent study of 1,300 patients, published in Brain Communications, found that while fewer vaccinated people had long COVID symptoms, being vaccinated prior to COVID-19 infection did not have much impact on neurologic long COVID symptoms (such as brain fog and headache). Whether someone is vaccinated or not, research does show that reinfection with COVID can trigger long COVID (or make it worse), as each episode puts a person at higher risk. And overall, long COVID prevalence and severity are similar across COVID variants.

“Maybe there’s damage to your lungs, or maybe your immune system is just lit on fire,” Levi says, or maybe it has something to do with your gut microbiome. Some research, he knew, has shown that gut health is linked to long COVID. A study using machine learning associated symptoms of long COVID, like abdominal pain and bloating, with the microbiome.

December 2023

AFTER DR. LIGHT received his diagnosis, he started trying to control what he could: drinking more water, swimming, stretching. Life changes that felt manageable. Like so many long COVID patients, he battled chronic fatigue and, if he overdid it mentally or physically at work or with exercise, experienced post-exertional malaise (when symptoms worsen after mild exertion). Just 25 minutes on the Peloton could feel good in the moment but overwhelm his body, leading to a crash, the worst of it sometimes occurring the next day. “Sleep doesn’t help,” Dr. Light says. “It can last for a few days and then lightens up again. It fluctuates.”

He read a study in Nature on people with post-COVID chronic fatigue syndrome that tested their blood and muscle before and after exercising. He found it reassuring that those who pushed themselves too hard in workouts didn’t appear to have more muscle damage. Some long COVID patients just can’t exert themselves, he says, because they have autonomic dysfunction—so their heart rate might get out of control when they exercise. Dealing with the condition is about paying attention to your body.

At this point, however, the mainstream medical community still hadn’t agreed on any treatment. In July 2023, two months after President Biden declared an end to COVID as a national emergency and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention discontinued publishing COVID-19 case data, the NIH opened enrollment for phase 2 clinical trials. These trials would evaluate at least four potential treatments for long COVID, with several more expected in the coming months, based on findings from the RECOVER Initiative (though some critics argued that the NIH had already fumbled the program, from its selection of scientists to its focus on “observational research” rather than treatments). The trials would test drugs, biologics, medical devices, and other therapies, like a longer regimen of the antiviral Paxlovid to see if it improved symptoms, brain training to boost cognitive function, wakefulness-promoting drugs for excessive daytime sleepiness, and a drug used to treat chronic heart failure for people with autonomic nervous system issues.

In the meantime, a colleague of Dr. Light’s who also has long COVID shared some research about naltrexone, which has typically been used to treat substance use disorders, like alcohol or opiate abuse. Naltrexone blocks opiate receptors, which regulate pain, reward, and addiction centers in the body. Addicts who take typical doses of naltrexone can’t get high anymore. A low dose of the drug has been used to relieve pain in autoimmune diseases such as Crohn’s and fibromyalgia.

Research into its effectiveness on long COVID remains thin, with clinical trials underway in Australia and Canada. But naltrexone is also affordable, safe, and well tolerated by patients, Dr. Light says, with very few side effects. “By blocking your opiate receptors just a little bit, it stimulates your body to make its own endogenous opiates, or feel-good hormones, so it can really help with fatigue and brain fog.”

He knows this as a pulmonologist who now prescribes—and takes—low-dose naltrexone, between one and six milligrams. “It’s not a fix,” he says, but the drug has had a measurable impact on his own fatigue. When he has stopped taking it for periods, he’s noticed that his symptoms return.

February 2024

AFTER A LONG YEAR of living with long COVID in a society that had pretty much moved on from the pandemic, Dr. Light posed a simple question to himself: Who is supposed to take care of long COVID patients? A family medicine doctor? An internal medicine doctor? A pulmonologist? As a patient himself, he experienced firsthand how hard it is to understand the condition: “Your whole body betrays you. But to everyone else, you look okay.” Dr. Light decided that since he had lots of experience with long COVID and was already a trained pulmonologist who treats breathing and sleep issues, he would be a good place to start.

He opened his clinic to more long COVID patients, and as word spread, they began flocking. Primary care doctors and other pulmonologists across the region increasingly referred their patients to him. Dr. Light, who considers his own symptoms, now more periodic, to be milder and more manageable than those of many of the patients he has met in his clinic, divides long COVID into three main types based on its symptoms. “There are the patients who have breathing difficulty after COVID, and oftentimes that’s very poorly explained by any kind of standard tests—lung function test, CAT scans,” he says. “Then there’s two other flavors. One is neurocognitive—people with a lot of issues with their nervous system.” Those symptoms, he says, include brain fog, chronic fatigue syndrome, neuropathic pain, and headaches. This is the biggest group. “And then there’s the people with dysautonomia. They have a lot of issues with standing up, racing heart rate when they’re trying to do any activity, even at much lower levels than before.”

Dr. Light got to know so many long COVID patients with various symptoms and treatment needs that he realized it could be helpful for them to also meet and get to know each other. “We needed a forum to make sure people got good information and are doing things safely and are getting the treatment that they need. Not just kind of grasping at straws out of desperation.” There are people who will take advantage of long COVID patients, he says, trying to sell them unproven treatments or even suggesting treatments that might be risky. He has to sift through the noise for himself as well as for his patients.

Dr. Light and a couple of his colleagues who also have long COVID started the Long COVID Information, Discussion, and Support Group in February 2024, which quickly drew over 100 in-person and remote patients from Colorado and beyond. At each monthly group session, Dr. Light reviews the smorgasbord of emerging research, citing the pros and cons of each study. Then patients discuss their own symptoms and their approaches to managing them. As long as they’re being safe and smart, he is supportive. “A lot of people kind of shotgun it,” he says. “We try to be more specific and systematic: What symptoms are you trying to treat? What data is out there for this particular supplement? Do we think it’s effective?”

Nicotine patches started flying off store shelves after small studies showed improvements in cognitive function and energy in long COVID patients who used them. (The idea is that the virus might bind to nicotine cell receptors, which are involved in areas that long COVID also seems to affect.) But the evidence “was really weak,” Dr. Light says. One study “basically took a few people with long COVID and gave them nicotine and made some graphs about how their symptoms improved after they started. There were a lot of leaps there.” He continues, “It seems like some shoddy science. If you give a bunch of people who are tired a stimulant like nicotine, yeah, some of them are going to feel better.”

He worries about fads like triple anticoagulation therapy, in which people at home try taking aspirin, an antiplatelet medication, and blood thinners to try to dissolve blood clots—even though the medical community has yet to demonstrate whether that’s a key problem in long COVID. “That one is worrisome,” Dr. Light says, “because that could be really dangerous.”

Dr. Light is also concerned that people are overspending on ideas that don’t have a proven benefit. A patient requested a $1,000 immunology profile, which they heard is the “gold standard” for diagnosing long COVID, since routine lab tests commonly used by doctors often can’t identify the disease in patients. He tries to explain to people who are clearly struggling and seeking testing, “I think your money would be better spent somewhere else.”

He reminds them that often tests come back normal, and doctors don’t even know exactly what they’re looking for in the results. He asks patients, “What information are we going to gain from this?” If they’re still determined, Dr. Light tries to assist, while also steering them toward other options. “A lot of people pretend to be experts on long COVID or really get vocal about what they think works,” he says. “On social media, there’s more hearsay than there is actual data.”
