# Blu-ray: The Best-Sounding Music Format You Aren't Listening To

[Best-Sounding Music Format You](https://innate-signal.jcesqc8.com/)

Home theaters aren't just for watching--they're for listening, as well. And I mean real listening: the kind of all-encompassing, sweep-you-off-your-feet musical absorption that you haven't experienced since you saw the Boss, John Mayer, or the Stones thoroughly dismantle the stadium in your town. Surround sound is part of the equation, of course, but we really owe our gratitude to Blu-ray. This fast-expanding disc format is not only the preferred medium for delivering high-definition video, it's also becoming the best way to experience music from live concerts and studio productions. Simply put, Blu-ray audio is exponentially better than mp3 and iTunes, and it even trounces the sound from CDs and DVDs. Need convincing? Let's do the math: To realize the best quality from digital sound--whether it's delivered in basic stereo or full-blown, multichannel surround--you need the most accurate copy of the original analog recording, which is largely a result of the number of times the music is sampled per second and the amount of musical information contained in each sample. For instance, a CD has 16 data bits of information in each sample and it uses 44,100 samples per second; call it 16/44.1 in 2 channels. By comparison, Blu-ray holds 24 bit samples with 192,000 samples per second. (24/194 in up to 7.1 channels). There's still some compression that's necessary--given that video must be stored on the Blu-ray discs, too--but unlike older DVD formats, Blu-ray's new HD audio formats, Dolby TruHD and dts-HD Master Audio, are lossless and preserve all of the fine detail of the original signals. They also preserve all the dynamics that your system and ears can handle.

For example, I've compared Roy Orbison's Black and White Night on CD, DVD with DolbyDigital and dts sound, and, even SACD (Super Audio CD) with the new Blu-ray in lossless HD audio in 5.1. No one, except those who were present at the concert or who sat in the mastering studio, had ever really heard that miraculous 1987 concert until 2008 when the Blu-ray was released. And that's the music from a decades-old movie soundtrack--newer audio-only productions, like Tom Petty's album MOJO on Blu-ray, are so much more immediate, powerful, and immersive that even their CD counterparts sound anemic in comparison.

Do you already have a high quality home theater system? If your A/V receiver has an HDMI input, you're in great shape. All Blu-ray players will output HD audio and video over this single cable (see Men's Health: wires-unraveled) and, if your A/V receiver is pretty new (HDMI version 1.3 or more), you can just send any of the formats straight on through (bitstream). But not every A/V receiver will decode Dolby TruHD and dts-HD Master Audio, especially if it is more than a couple of years old. The solution is to set up your new Blu-ray player to decode those formats internally to what's called LPCM (there's an option for this in the player's setup menu), since any version of HDMI can handle that just fine.

If your A/V receiver lacks HDMI, you aren't shut out, but you may have to be a bit more inventive. Older pre-HDMI receivers always have analog audio inputs and most had multichannel analog inputs, an array of RCA jacks with one each for the front left and right, center, surround left and right, and subwoofer (LFE) channels. So you need a Blu-ray player that has the corresponding 6 RCA analog outputs. Unfortunately, these models are slowly disappearing from the shelves as everyone is moving to HDMI. But if you can find such a player--one that will also decode Dolby TruHD and dts-HD Master Audio--then you just have to buy 6 RCA interconnect cable and hook it up to your A/V receiver. (Note: Since these cables only carry audio, you'll also have to connect the RCA video cables for video.)

Only have a 2 channel audio system and not a home theater system? Get in the game with a Blu-ray player that has stereo RCA jacks. (The digital jacks are only good for the older Dolby Digital or dts formats, not formats needed for HD.) You'll still be enjoying HD audio, though you'll have to forego the full-surround stuff. That may not be a big issue for you as some of the best HD remasterings of classic stereo performances are still in stereo. Emblematic of this is the humongous Neil Young box set, Archives Vol. 1 1963-1972. The Blu-ray version is in 24/192 stereo LPCM but, regardless of the age of these cuts, they are amazingly clean and vibrant. Heck, the Blu-ray of Young's new album, Le Noise, is in 24/192 stereo LPCM, but it's a new production by famed U2 producer Daniel Lanois, and what you hear is tighter, closer and more thrilling than anything on the CD. You might just close your eyes, ignore that big screen and groove on the best-sounding music on the planet.
