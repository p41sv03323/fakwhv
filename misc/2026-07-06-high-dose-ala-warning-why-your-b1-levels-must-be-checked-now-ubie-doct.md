# High-Dose ALA Warning: Why Your B1 Levels Must Be Checked Now | Ubie Doctor's Note

Published on: 5/6/2026

Why Your Doctor Checks B1 Levels if You Take High-Dose ALA

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

High-dose Alpha Lipoic Acid (ALA) accelerates thiamine (vitamin B1) turnover and can deplete B1 stores, potentially causing fatigue, nerve dysfunction, and cardiovascular complications.

Doctors monitor B1 levels during ALA therapy to detect early depletion, optimize treatment, and guide dietary or supplement adjustments. Catching deficiency early helps prevent serious neurological and cardiac issues.

Because symptoms of B1 depletion—fatigue, tingling, weakness, or heart irregularities—overlap with many other conditions, it's important to identify what's really driving how you feel. What Is Alpha Lipoic Acid?

Alpha Lipoic Acid is a naturally occurring compound that acts as an antioxidant. You may see it recommended for:

Diabetic neuropathy – to help reduce nerve pain and improve nerve function

Blood sugar support – to enhance insulin sensitivity

General antioxidant support – protecting cells from oxidative stress

People take ALA both as a prescription medication (in some countries) and as an over-the-counter supplement. Typical daily doses range from 300 mg to 600 mg, but "high-dose" protocols may go up to 1,200 mg or more under medical supervision.

The Role of Thiamine (Vitamin B1)

Thiamine is an essential B-vitamin involved in:

Energy production – converting carbohydrates into usable energy (ATP)

Nervous system function – maintaining healthy nerve cells and neurotransmitter production

Heart health – supporting normal cardiac function

Your body cannot make thiamine on its own, so you must get it from food or supplements. Good dietary sources include whole grains, legumes, nuts, pork, and fortified cereals.

How High-Dose ALA and Thiamine Interact

Several credible studies and clinical observations show that taking high-dose ALA can influence thiamine metabolism and increase your risk of thiamine deficiency:

Increased thiamine turnover: ALA enhances cellular energy production. That extra metabolic "turnover" can use up thiamine more rapidly.

Competitive enzyme reactions: Both ALA and thiamine are involved in the same enzyme-driven pathways (e.g., pyruvate dehydrogenase). High levels of one may alter the efficiency of the other.

Potential depletion: Over weeks or months of high-dose ALA, without adequate thiamine intake, stores can decline.

When thiamine levels drop too low, you risk impaired energy production, nerve dysfunction, and even more serious complications.

Why Doctors Monitor B1 Levels on High-Dose ALA

Your healthcare provider orders B1 blood tests to:

Ensure safety: Detect early depletion before symptoms appear.

Optimize therapy: Confirm you have enough thiamine for energy metabolism, maximizing the benefits of ALA.

Prevent complications: Avoid nerve damage or cardiac effects from severe deficiency.

Monitoring typically involves a simple blood draw to measure thiamine diphosphate (the active form) in red blood cells. Results guide decisions on whether to adjust your diet, start a thiamine supplement, or alter your ALA dose.

Balancing ALA and Thiamine: Practical Tips

To support both ALA benefits and healthy thiamine levels:

Eat thiamine-rich foods daily:

Whole grains (brown rice, oats)

Legumes (beans, lentils)

Pork or lean meats

Nuts and seeds

Consider a B-complex supplement if your diet is limited. A typical dose provides 1.2–1.5 mg of thiamine, covering the daily requirement.

Stay hydrated and maintain a balanced diet; deficiencies often co-exist.

If you're on a restricted diet (e.g., low-carb, vegan), be extra vigilant about B1 sources.

Track your ALA dose and discuss any increases with your doctor.
