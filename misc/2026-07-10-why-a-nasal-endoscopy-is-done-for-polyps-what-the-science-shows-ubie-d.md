Explanation

Nasal polyps are noncancerous, swollen growths that can develop in the lining of your nasal passages or sinuses. They often result from chronic inflammation due to asthma, allergies, sinus infections or immune disorders. While some small polyps may not cause noticeable symptoms, larger or multiple polyps can lead to:

Nasal congestion or blockage

Reduced sense of smell or taste

Facial pain or pressure

Chronic sinus infections

When these symptoms persist despite medication, your doctor may recommend a nasal endoscopy. This minimally invasive procedure provides a clear, magnified view of your nasal passages and sinuses to confirm or rule out polyps, evaluate their size and plan treatment.

What Is Nasal Endoscopy?

A nasal endoscopy uses a thin, flexible tube (endoscope) equipped with a light and camera. Your ENT (ear, nose and throat) specialist inserts it through your nostril to examine areas that are hard to see with a standard exam. Key features include:

High-definition camera for detailed imagery

Why Nasal Endoscopy for Polyp Visualization Is Essential

Research shows nasal endoscopy outperforms simple anterior rhinoscopy (using a light and speculum) in detecting polyps and accurately determining their size and location. Benefits include:

Treatment Planning: Knowing exact polyp size and attachment points guides decisions on medications, steroid sprays or surgery.

Monitoring Progress: Follow-up endoscopies track how well treatments shrink or remove polyps.

Minimally Invasive: Generally performed in an outpatient setting under local anesthesia or light sedation.

Endoscopy detects up to 90% of nasal polyps, compared to about 60% with anterior rhinoscopy.

The Lund–Kennedy scoring system, used during endoscopy, grades polyps and inflammation to guide therapy.

In many cases, endoscopy provides enough detail to avoid radiation exposure from CT imaging.

When surgery is needed, preoperative endoscopy maps anatomy, reducing complications and recurrence rates.

The Procedure: What to Expect

You may receive a decongestant spray or topical anesthetic to numb the nasal lining.

You'll be seated. The doctor gently inserts the endoscope into each nostril, asking you to breathe normally.

You might feel slight pressure or the urge to sneeze, but pain is minimal.

Typically 5–10 minutes.

Mild nasal irritation or watery eyes for a few hours.

Normal activities can usually resume immediately.

Insurance Coverage and Financial Assistance

Most insurance plans cover nasal endoscopy when deemed medically necessary for diagnosis or treatment planning of nasal polyps. To ensure coverage:

Obtain a referral from your primary care doctor

Confirm preauthorization requirements with your insurer

Submit documentation of persistent symptoms and prior treatments

If you're uninsured or underinsured, ask about payment plans or sliding-scale fees.

Risks and Safety

Nasal endoscopy is generally safe, but like any procedure, it carries minimal risks:

Mild bleeding or nosebleed

Temporary nasal discomfort or swelling

Rarely, infection

Your specialist will review these risks and ensure the benefits outweigh potential complications.
