Explanation

A calm, steady heartbeat at night is a sign that your body is resting and repairing itself. For many seniors, however, the heart may seem to race just when it's time to sleep. If you've been wondering how to lower a high resting heart rate at night, you're not alone.

A normal resting heart rate for most adults ranges between 60 and 100 beats per minute (bpm). Many healthy older adults fall between 60 and 80 bpm at rest. During sleep, heart rate typically drops even lower. If your pulse regularly stays high at night—or suddenly jumps—it's worth paying attention.

Let's walk through what causes this, what you can safely do about it, and when to speak to a doctor.

Why Does Heart Rate Increase at Night?

Your heart rate is controlled by your nervous system. At night, the body should shift into "rest and repair" mode. When it doesn't, several factors may be involved:

Stress or anxiety

Poor sleep or insomnia

Dehydration

Caffeine or alcohol use

Certain medications

Pain

Fever or infection

Thyroid problems

Heart rhythm disorders (such as atrial fibrillation)

In seniors, nighttime heart rate changes can also be linked to sleep apnea, a condition where breathing briefly stops during sleep. This can cause the heart to work harder.

A single episode of a faster pulse may not be serious. But frequent or persistent episodes deserve attention.

How to Lower a High Resting Heart Rate at Night

The good news is that many nighttime heart rate issues can improve with practical, healthy changes.

1. Improve Your Sleep Routine

Better sleep habits can directly lower your nighttime pulse.

Try these steps:

Go to bed and wake up at the same time daily.

Keep your bedroom cool, dark, and quiet.

Avoid screens (TV, phone, tablet) at least 1 hour before bed.

Limit stimulating conversations or stressful activities at night.

A consistent routine trains your nervous system to relax.

2. Avoid Stimulants in the Evening

Stimulants can quietly raise your heart rate for hours.

Limit or avoid after mid-afternoon:

Coffee

Tea (including green tea)

Soda

Chocolate

Nicotine

Alcohol may make you sleepy at first, but it can increase heart rate later in the night. Reducing evening alcohol can make a noticeable difference.

3. Stay Properly Hydrated

Dehydration forces your heart to pump harder. Seniors are more prone to dehydration because thirst sensation decreases with age.

To support a healthy resting heart rate:

Drink water steadily throughout the day.

Limit excessive alcohol.

Speak to your doctor if you take diuretics ("water pills").

Avoid drinking large amounts right before bed to prevent sleep disruption.

4. Practice Gentle Relaxation Before Bed

Calming your nervous system lowers your pulse.

Helpful techniques include:

Inhale for 4 seconds

Hold for 4 seconds

Exhale for 6 seconds

Repeat for 5–10 minutes

Light stretching

Listening to calming music

Guided relaxation recordings

Deep breathing stimulates the vagus nerve, which helps slow heart rate naturally.

5. Get Regular Daytime Exercise

Regular physical activity strengthens the heart, allowing it to pump more efficiently and beat slower at rest.

Safe options for most seniors include:

Walking

Swimming

Chair exercises

Light strength training

Tai chi or gentle yoga

Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week, if approved by your doctor.

Avoid intense exercise close to bedtime, as it may temporarily raise heart rate.

6. Maintain a Heart-Healthy Diet

What you eat affects your heart rhythm.

Focus on:

Vegetables and fruits

Whole grains

Lean proteins

Healthy fats (olive oil, nuts, fish)

Limit:

Excess salt

Sugary foods

Processed meals

A balanced diet helps control blood pressure and inflammation, both of which affect heart rate.

7. Review Your Medications

Some medications can increase heart rate, including:

Decongestants

Certain asthma inhalers

Thyroid medications (if dose is too high)

Some antidepressants

Never stop medication on your own. But if you notice changes in your nighttime pulse after starting a new medicine, speak to your doctor.

8. Check for Sleep Apnea

If you experience:

Loud snoring

Pauses in breathing during sleep

Waking up gasping

Morning headaches

Excessive daytime sleepiness

Sleep apnea may be raising your heart rate at night. Treatment can significantly reduce cardiovascular strain.

When a Fast Nighttime Heart Rate Is More Serious

Sometimes a high resting heart rate at night signals a medical condition.

Seek prompt medical care if you experience:

Chest pain or pressure

Shortness of breath

Fainting or near fainting

Severe dizziness

New confusion

Heart rate consistently above 100 bpm at rest

These symptoms can indicate a potentially life-threatening condition and require immediate evaluation.

Monitoring Your Heart Rate Safely

Many seniors use:

Wrist fitness trackers

Home blood pressure monitors

Pulse oximeters

These tools can be helpful, but avoid checking obsessively. Checking repeatedly can increase anxiety, which may actually raise your heart rate.

Instead:

Measure once before bed.

Measure once if you wake feeling symptoms.

Keep a simple log to share with your doctor.

Patterns matter more than one isolated reading.

Managing Anxiety About Heart Rate

It's natural to worry about your heart. But constant worry activates the stress response, increasing pulse further.

If anxiety is contributing:

Practice slow breathing daily.

Limit late-night news or stimulating media.

Talk to a counselor if needed.

Discuss anxiety treatment options with your doctor.

A calm mind supports a calm heart.

The Bottom Line

If you're wondering how to lower a high resting heart rate at night, focus on:

Better sleep habits

Reducing evening stimulants

Staying hydrated

Gentle relaxation techniques

Regular daytime exercise

Heart-healthy nutrition

Reviewing medications

Screening for sleep apnea

Most nighttime pulse increases are manageable with these steps. However, persistent or worsening symptoms should never be ignored.

Your heart works hard for you every day. If your nighttime pulse is consistently high, irregular, or accompanied by concerning symptoms, speak to a doctor promptly. Some causes are harmless, but others can be serious or life threatening if left untreated.

Taking action early—calmly and thoughtfully—is the best way to protect your heart and your peace of mind.
