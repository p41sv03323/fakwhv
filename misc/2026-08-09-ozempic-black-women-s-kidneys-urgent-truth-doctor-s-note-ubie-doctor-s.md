Doctor's Note: Does Ozempic Protect or Hurt Black Women's Kidneys? A Doctor Sets the Record Straight

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) disproportionately affects Black women, driven largely by higher rates of type 2 diabetes and hypertension. As many turn to newer therapies like Ozempic (semaglutide) to manage blood sugar and weight, questions arise about the impact of these drugs on kidney health. Below, we'll explore what the science says about Ozempic and kidney health in Black women—balancing potential benefits with possible risks—and offer practical steps to protect your kidneys.

What Is Ozempic?Ozempic is a once-weekly injectable medication in the GLP-1 receptor agonist class. It helps:

• Lower blood sugar by boosting insulin release when glucose is high• Slow down stomach emptying, reducing appetite• Promote weight loss

Because diabetes and obesity are leading causes of CKD, Ozempic has become popular among people looking to control their glucose and shed pounds.

Why Focus on Black Women's Kidneys?Black women face a higher lifetime risk of CKD and kidney failure than many other groups. Contributing factors include:

• Genetic predispositions (e.g., APOL1 gene variants)• Greater prevalence of hypertension and diabetes• Socioeconomic disparities affecting access to care

Getting the right diabetes medicine can be a game-changer for slowing kidney damage. But is Ozempic part of that solution—or could it introduce new kidney concerns?

Potential Benefits of Ozempic on Kidney HealthEvidence suggests Ozempic and other GLP-1 receptor agonists may slow CKD progression, mainly by improving diabetes control and reducing weight:

• Better blood sugar levels: Lower A1C reduces the burden on tiny kidney blood vessels.• Reduced blood pressure: Weight loss and improved vascular function can ease hypertension.• Decreased albuminuria: Studies report lower protein leakage in urine, an early sign of kidney stress.

Large clinical trials that included participants of varied backgrounds showed:

Potential Risks and ConcernsWhile the data on Ozempic and kidney health are overall positive, some caveats apply:

• Dehydration risk. Nausea, vomiting or diarrhea can lead to fluid loss, potentially triggering acute kidney injury (AKI).• Rare AKI reports. A small number of case studies describe sudden kidney function decline, often in the setting of severe GI side effects.• Medication interactions. Certain painkillers (NSAIDs) and diuretics may compound kidney stress if hydration is poor.

What the Trials Tell UsThe SUSTAIN and REWIND trials—large, randomized studies of GLP-1 receptor agonists—offer the best data:

• SUSTAIN 6 (semaglutide): Showed a 36% reduction in new-onset persistent macroalbuminuria (a marker of kidney damage).• REWIND (dulaglutide, a sister drug): Demonstrated a 15% lower risk of a composite kidney endpoint (including decline in kidney function and need for dialysis).

Although these studies weren't exclusive to Black women, subgroup analyses didn't raise red flags for any particular race or sex. Real-world registries are emerging that suggest similar benefits across diverse populations, including Black women.

Practical Tips for Protecting Your Kidneys on OzempicIf you and your doctor decide Ozempic is right for you, consider these steps to safeguard kidney health:

• Stay well-hydrated. Sip water throughout the day, especially if you experience nausea or diarrhea.• Monitor kidney function. Ask your provider for regular blood tests (e.g., serum creatinine, eGFR) and urine checks for protein.• Manage blood pressure. Keep hypertension in check with lifestyle measures and, if needed, medicines your doctor prescribes.• Report GI issues early. If you develop severe nausea, vomiting or diarrhea, call your provider to adjust dosage or receive supportive care.• Review all medicines. Let your healthcare team know about over-the-counter pain relievers or supplements you use.

Early detection of changes in kidney function allows for quick action, helping to prevent long-term damage.
