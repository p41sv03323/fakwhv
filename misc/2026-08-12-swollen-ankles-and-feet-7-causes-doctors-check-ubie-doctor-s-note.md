Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Swollen ankles and feet (peripheral edema) can result from seven main causes: injury, blood clots (DVT), chronic venous insufficiency, heart failure, kidney disease, liver disease, lymphatic disorders, or side effects from medications. Pinpointing the underlying cause is essential, because each condition requires different diagnostic tests and treatments.

Symptoms vary widely—some causes trigger sudden, painful swelling in one leg (a potential medical emergency), while others develop gradually in both legs alongside fatigue, shortness of breath, or skin changes. Because the right next step depends on your specific symptoms, medical history, and risk factors, a personalized assessment is the fastest way to clarify what may be going on.

Swollen Ankles and Feet: 7 Causes Doctors Check

Swollen ankles and feet are common complaints that can range from harmless to serious. Fluid can build up in your tissues for many reasons—from standing too long to underlying health issues. Understanding the possible causes helps you and your doctor figure out the best next steps. Below are seven causes doctors often investigate when you have persistent or unexplained swelling in your lower legs.

1. Injury or Trauma

Sprains, strains and fractures around the ankle joint or foot often cause rapid-onset swelling.

Pain when you put weight on the foot

Bruising, redness or warmth over the injured area

Limited range of motion

[Ankles and Feet](https://lumina-dermis.sunsimiaosd.com/)

Physical exam checking stability of ligaments

X-rays to rule out fractures

Rest, ice, compression and elevation (the RICE method)

Over-the-counter pain relievers

Physical therapy if needed

Most injuries improve in days to weeks. If swelling worsens or you can't move your ankle at all, see a doctor promptly.

2. Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

A blood clot in a deep leg vein can block circulation and lead to one-sided swelling.

Recent surgery or long hospital stays

Prolonged immobility (long flights, bed rest)

Certain birth control pills or smoking

Swelling in one leg only

Pain, tenderness or cramping in the calf

Skin that feels warm and looks red

Ultrasound of the leg veins

D-dimer blood test to check for clotting activity

Blood thinners (anticoagulants)

Compression stockings to improve flow

[Doctor's Note](https://leanorbit.dns0663.com/)

Monitoring for signs of clot moving to the lungs

DVT is potentially life-threatening if untreated. Always seek medical attention if you suspect a clot.

3. Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)

When leg veins can't pump blood back up effectively, fluid pools in your ankles and feet.

Older adults

People who stand or sit for hours

Those with a history of leg injury or clot

Bilateral swelling that worsens by day's end

Achy or heavy legs

Varicose veins, skin discoloration or ulcers

Duplex ultrasound to assess venous flow

Physical exam of skin changes

Elevating legs above heart level several times daily

Graduated compression stockings

Exercise to strengthen calf muscles

In some cases, minimally invasive vein procedures

With proper care, CVI symptoms often improve and complications can be minimized.

4. Heart Failure

When the heart can't pump efficiently, fluid may back up into the legs.

Shortness of breath with minimal exertion

Fatigue, racing heartbeat or persistent cough

Elevated jugular venous pressure in the neck

Echocardiogram (heart ultrasound)

Blood tests (BNP or NT-proBNP levels)

Chest X-ray to check for fluid in lungs

Diuretics ("water pills") to reduce excess fluid

Medications to strengthen heart function

Lifestyle changes: low-salt diet, fluid restriction, regular exercise

Early detection and treatment can slow progression and improve quality of life.

5. Kidney Disease

Damaged kidneys can't remove excess fluid and salt, leading to swelling.

Swelling around the eyes in the morning

Changes in urine output or color

High blood pressure

Blood tests for creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)

Urinalysis to check for protein or blood

Kidney ultrasound in some cases

Controlling blood pressure (ACE inhibitors or ARBs)

Diuretics to help eliminate extra fluid

Diet low in salt and protein, tailored by a dietitian

Managing kidney health early can prevent serious complications like fluid overload.

6. Liver Disease

Severe liver conditions reduce production of albumin, a protein that keeps fluid in blood vessels.

Yellowing of skin or eyes (jaundice)

Abdominal swelling (ascites)

Easy bruising or bleeding

Liver function tests (ALT, AST, bilirubin)

Ultrasound or CT scan of the liver

Sometimes a liver biopsy

Addressing the underlying liver disease (e.g., antiviral for hepatitis)

Low-salt diet and diuretics to control fluid

Monitoring for complications like variceal bleeding

Early referral to a liver specialist (hepatologist) can greatly improve outcomes.

7. Lymphatic and Other Causes

When lymph vessels are blocked or overwhelmed, fluid collecting in the tissues causes swelling (lymphedema). Some medications and nutritional issues can also play a role. A feeling of heaviness or tightness in the limb

Pitting (indentation) that may become non-pitting over time

History of cancer treatment or surgery near lymph nodes

Calcium channel blockers (for high blood pressure)

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

Certain diabetes treatments

Lymphoscintigraphy or MRI lymphangiography

Manual lymphatic drainage and compression bandaging

Reviewing and adjusting medications with your doctor

Often, simple changes—like switching medications—can ease swelling significantly.

When to See a Doctor

Most mild ankle and foot swelling improves with home care: elevation, gentle movement, low-salt diet and compression socks. However, contact a healthcare provider if you experience:

Sudden or severe swelling

One-sided swelling, pain or redness (possible DVT)

Shortness of breath, chest pain or lightheadedness

Swelling plus fever, chills or signs of infection

Facial or abdominal swelling

Always speak to a doctor about anything that could be life threatening or serious.

Taking a proactive role—tracking symptoms, noting triggers and sharing this information—will help your doctor arrive at the right diagnosis sooner. With the right evaluation, treatment and lifestyle changes, most causes of swollen ankles and feet respond well.
