Understanding Egg Allergy Symptoms in Adults: Doctor Testing and Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Egg allergy in adults typically causes skin, gastrointestinal, or respiratory reactions within minutes to hours of eating egg proteins. Diagnosis relies on a detailed medical history, skin prick testing, and specific IgE blood tests, with an oral food challenge used when results are unclear. Effective management includes strict egg avoidance through careful label reading, preventing cross-contamination in food preparation, making nutritional adjustments to replace lost protein and nutrients, carrying an epinephrine auto-injector for emergencies, and scheduling regular follow-up visits with an allergist.

Because symptoms of egg allergy can overlap with food intolerances, other allergies, or unrelated conditions, identifying the true cause matters. Egg allergy, while more common in children, can start or persist into adulthood. Common Egg Allergy Symptoms in Adults

Egg allergy involves an immune response to proteins in egg whites or yolks. Symptoms often appear shortly after eating eggs or foods containing egg. Adults may notice:

Skin reactions

Hives (raised, red, itchy bumps)

Eczema flare-ups

Swelling of lips, face or eyelids

Gastrointestinal discomfort

Nausea or vomiting

Abdominal cramps

Diarrhea

Respiratory issues

Note: Mild symptoms can escalate. Always treat any breathing difficulty, fainting or swelling of the tongue and throat as a medical emergency—call 911 or your local emergency number immediately.

Timing of Symptoms

Most egg allergy reactions in adults occur within minutes to two hours of exposure. A few people experience delayed symptoms up to several hours later, especially gastrointestinal signs. Tracking when you eat eggs and when symptoms begin can help your doctor pinpoint the cause.

When to See a Doctor

If you suspect an egg allergy, schedule an appointment if you experience:

Repeated skin or digestive symptoms after eating egg-containing foods

Any respiratory symptoms (wheezing, coughing, nasal congestion)

Symptoms worsening over time or spreading to multiple body systems

Signs of anaphylaxis, even if they resolve after taking antihistamines

Early evaluation reduces risk and helps you learn safe eating strategies.

Next Steps After Diagnosis

Egg Avoidance Strategies

Read ingredient labels carefully for eggs, albumin, globulin, lysozyme and other egg derivatives.

Watch for hidden eggs in baked goods, pasta, meatloaf, mayonnaise, sauces, and some cosmetics.

Prevent cross-contamination: use separate utensils, rinse cutting boards and wipe counters after preparing egg dishes.

Nutritional Considerations

Eggs are a good source of protein, vitamins and minerals. Avoidance may require adjustments:

Choose alternative protein sources: poultry, fish, beans, lentils, tofu, nuts or seeds.

Consider fortified dairy or plant-based milks for calcium, vitamin D and B12.

If you're concerned about nutrient gaps, discuss supplementation or a referral to a registered dietitian.

Emergency Preparedness

If you've ever had or are at risk for a severe reaction:

Carry an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen®, Auvi-Q® or similar) at all times.

Learn how and when to use it.

Have a written emergency action plan.

Inform family, friends and coworkers about your allergy and what to do in an emergency.

Monitoring and Follow-Up

Keep a symptom diary for at least a month, even if reactions are mild.

Schedule regular follow-up visits to review avoidance measures, auto-injector use and any new symptoms.

Some adults may outgrow mild egg allergies over years, but this is less common than in children.
