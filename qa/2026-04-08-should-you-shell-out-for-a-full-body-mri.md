# Should You Shell Out for a Full-Body MRI?

[Out for Full-Body MRI](https://memorycircuits.hbsnzj.com/)

In August, Kim Kardashian and designer Zac Posen each posted photos on Instagram wearing dark gray scrubs while sitting on the bright blue bed of an MRI machine. These weren’t doctor-ordered visits to investigate a worrisome lump or a persistent headache. Both Kardashian and Posen were getting full-body MRI scans at Prenuvo, a Redwood City-based company with locations around North America—scans that cost $2,499 for your full body (or $1,799 for head and torso and $999 for just the torso).

The procedure is marketed as a non-invasive, total-body service that can check for hidden cancer, aneurysms, and cysts, among other hidden menaces that might keep you up at night. Alongside Prenuvo, companies like Ezra and SimonMed have moved into the “wellness MRI” market.

As many Instagram commenters quickly pointed out, though, this type of out-of-pocket scan is out of reach of most Americans, many of whom struggle to pay for even basic healthcare. (Just one remark: “wow thanks so much for sharing something 90 percent of the population can’t afford.”) While Kardashian claimed her post was #notanad, Posen's had a discount code, so it’s not totally clear if the celebrities are being driven by endorsements or an interest in wellness.

Doctors worry these scans might lead to unnecessary tests and even surgeries, when most of the public would be better off focusing on lifestyle changes to prevent cancer, delay heart disease, and improve longevity, and use targeted MRI scans only to address specific concerns. Still, the promise of detecting deadly disease early is intriguing.

So is it worth the price?

What a Wellness MRI Actually Is

[Should You Shell](https://regeneration-desk.hbsnzj.com/blog/8921253578.html)

The full-body MRI at Prenuvo takes an hour, and the experience is just like that of any other MRIs—it involves lying completely still in a small tube while the machine makes loud banging, clicking, clanging, and whirring sounds. There's no radiation involved, so the scan itself poses no health risk. Results from the company arrive in about a week, and if anything looks serious, Prenuvo alerts your doctor and may recommend a specialist.

Andrew Lacy, the CEO of Prenuvo, founded the company in 2018, teaming up with radiologist and biomedical engineer Raj Attariwala, M.D. Lacy says he had the idea earlier that year when he looked at himself in the mirror and realized he had no idea what was going on inside of his body. He wanted to answer the question: “Am I okay? Is there anything I need to know about what’s inside of me?” He found Dr. Attariwala, who was doing full-body MRIs in Vancouver for people who had a family history of cancer or who were curious about their health, and got a scan.

“I learned more about my health after a one-hour scan than the health system had told me my entire life,” Lacy says. When he got his own full-body scan, he didn’t discover any life-threatening tumors or a looming aneurysm, but he did feel a sense of relief he describes as euphoric.

Lacy says these scans are particularly helpful for men, who, as he says, “suck at preventative health.” Men in general tend to ignore their symptoms and engage in high-risk lifestyle behaviors and as a result, cancer is diagnosed at a higher rate in men than women. Lacy says his company finds cancer in about 4 to 5 percent of the people scanned and aneurysms in 1 to 1.5 percent of patients. (Keep in mind that the people who’ve had these scans aren’t a randomized, controlled group, so those numbers might not apply to the general population.)

That said, these scans can’t check for everything. They're not useful in detecting blocked arteries in the heart or determining the densities of coronary artery calcifications—that's what a CT scan is for.

One and…Not Done?

One of the more perplexing things about the buzz around these scans is that they don’t exempt you from the usual screenings or doctor visits. Prenuvo advises patients to continue getting colonoscopies and skin-cancer checks, because physicians have tools (scopes, for one) that are more precise than any type of imaging, Lacy says. Plus, the full-body MRI isn't great at finding colon, thyroid or breast cancers.

So then what’s your $2,499 doing for you? Ideally, you're checking for something that wouldn't get caught in a regular, targeted screening, or that hasn't grown enough to cause pain or a visible tumor. Even if you have lingering aches and pains, Prenuvo states that its service can help find the cause. The company also states that its scans can help assess general health as well as recommend lifestyle changes to make health improvements. And If you don’t find anything, the scan might offer peace of mind, at least for a year or two.

But then there's what happens when the scan discovers a cyst, lump, or other worrisome result. While Lacy says full-body MRIs can free you from thinking about your health, it’s very possible the procedure could uncover something that will make you suddenly extremely preoccupied with your health—sending you down a rabbit hole of costly tests, biopsies, even surgeries. For instance, the MRI can’t tell you if a spot is cancerous or not; you'll still need a biopsy to confirm what the scan suggests.

Radiologists and cancer specialists don’t object to the MRI itself, but neither the National Cancer Institute nor the American College of Radiology recommends whole body MRI screenings for people without symptoms, warning they could lead to overdiagnosis. “You find a non-cancerous thing that you would have lived with throughout your life, without any issues,” says Dakshesh Patel, M.D., a radiologist who is a medical director at the Keck School of Medicine HCCII Imaging Center of the University of Southern California. This then requires a biopsy or other invasive procedures, which have risk factors, take up your time, and cause stress. These procedures, though, can also come back positive or problematic, in which case they could save your life. Which is not nothing.

The other drawback with a whole-body MRI, Dr. Patel says, is that it is only a snapshot in time. While you might get the all-clear that day, the scans have to be repeated every year or two. That could add up to something like $12,500 to $25,000 per decade.

"If you do not really have any symptoms, like pain or like you feel a mass or anything like that, I would suggest not to get the MRI scan done," says Dr. Patel. Even if "everyone" on social media is signing up.
