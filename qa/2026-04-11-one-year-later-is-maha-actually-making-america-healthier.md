# One Year Later: Is MAHA Actually Making America Healthier?

AT HIS NOMINATION hearing in January 2025, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced he’d “do everything in my power to put the health of Americans back on track.” And in the year since then, he’s made some bold changes under the banner of the MAHA movement. MH takes a close look at what they’re doing to our health.

THE MOVE:

“Cutting the Fat” from Medical Research & Public Health

MILLIONS OF DOLLARS in research grants have been cut or frozen. An estimated 20,000 workers at HHS have left or been forced out. Until now, the National Institutes of Health has been the biggest single public funder of biomedical and behavioral research in the world. The HHS budget request for next year shrinks NIH funding by about 40 percent. Lawmakers have pushed back against the cuts—the latest Congressional proposals do not dramatically slash NIH funding—but there could still be a repeat of 2025, with the White House not spending funds Congress authorizes.

Are We Healthier?

No. The personnel losses include civil servants who act as our eyes and ears inside the factories where drugs are manufactured, where meat and seafood are processed, where infant formula is made. These are the people who monitor public health and investigate illness outbreaks. They perform the kinds of jobs no one notices—until the work doesn’t get done. “This is a problem public health has always had,” says Jerome M. Adams, MD, of Purdue University, who was U.S. surgeon general in the first Trump administration. “When your water is clean and you’re not getting sick from it, when the air is safe to breathe, you don’t notice it. But you do notice that when these systems that have taken decades to build up go away.”

The retreat from investing in research could be felt for decades. “People will die earlier than they otherwise would,” says Scott W. Delaney, a former Harvard University researcher who now helps run a tracker for canceled research grants. “We will have fewer tools in the toolbox to treat diseases and fewer tools in the toolbox to keep people healthy.”

Even if Congress keeps the draconian cuts at bay, harm has already been done, says Lawrence O. Gostin, a professor of global health law at Georgetown Law. Laboratories have closed and a lot of research can’t just pick up where it left off (e.g., you can’t go back and get a blood sample from six weeks ago). Canceled NIH grants include ones to study circadian rhythms and Alzheimer’s risk, suicide risk among Black Americans, and new ways to treat prostate cancer.

Changes in Vaccine Research & Policy

MONTHS AFTER KENNEDY took office, the HHS fired all members of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), which guides vaccine policy at the CDC. The reconstituted ACIP recently voted to recommend separating the chickenpox vaccine from the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine, now combined into one shot called MMR, and slashed the number of vaccines recommended for children. In addition, the HHS under Kennedy canceled research programs involving mRNA technology, which was credited with saving millions of lives during the COVID-19 pandemic.

No. These moves sow doubt in the safety and effectiveness of vaccines. Even what seems like a tweak in the MMR recommendation “makes it look like something unsavory,” says Peter J. Hotez, MD, codirector of the Texas Children’s Hospital Center for Vaccine Development. A survey released by the Annenberg Public Policy Center found that public support for the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine dropped from 90 percent to 82 percent in 2025. Lower immunization rates clear the way for more infection. In 2025, more than 2.200 Americans got measles, more than in any year in the past three decades. Two children and one unvaccinated adult died—the first fatalities from measles in a decade and the first pediatric deaths from the disease since 2003.

Measles will likely not be the only preventable disease to surge, the experts we talked to warn. Kennedy’s handpicked appointees to ACIP include people who have spoken skeptically of vaccines. One is a board member of a nonprofit who platforms vaccine misinformation. “I think that we have to acknowledge that consistent misinformation has real consequences,” says Dr. Adams, “and so when people are delaying routine vaccinations, we’re going to see those diseases come back.”

In addition, quashing mRNA research programs, which are also promising for cancer treatment, isn’t making America healthier, according to our experts. Instead, it leaves the country vulnerable to anything unexpected. “Right now, in my view, we have no public health preparedness, no pandemic preparedness,” says Dr. Hotez. “So if we get another major coronavirus, or a virus like an Ebola, or if we have yellow fever coming back to the Gulf Coast, or if there’s a bioterrorist event, there’s absolutely nothing in place” to stop it. Dr. Adams is further concerned that moves like these “promote a romanticized view of the past that ignores the lifesaving impact of long-standing public health measures,” he says. “Suggesting Americans were broadly healthier before these interventions—and can be made healthy ‘again’ by eschewing them—is not only historically inaccurate but risks undermining trust in the very tools that have saved millions of lives.”

Focus on Physical Activity & Chronic Disease

PRESIDENT TRUMP ISSUED an executive order to reestablish the Presidential Fitness Test, which traces back to the 1950s. A Make Our Children Healthy Again report promised to work with schools on an awareness campaign that would help increase physical activity.

We could be, if this goes beyond gym class. The Presidential Fitness Test helps put physical activity in the spotlight in a country where currently less than a third of kids get even an hour of daily exercise. But the test itself has been empowering for some and a humiliating public struggle and turnoff for others. A more modern approach would give kids the tools they need to exercise for life—ones that go beyond motivation help or workout moves.

Even if people want to move, they often live where it’s not safe to go out and walk, Dr. Adams points out. Neighborhoods with sidewalks, well-lighted streets, hiking trails, bike paths, and public transit are key. “If we can put some funding into our cities, that could make a big difference in improving the health of our nation,” says Stella L. Volpe, PhD, RDN, head of the department of human nutrition, foods, and exercise at Virginia Tech. Starting in 2023, some municipalities received infrastructure improvement grants through the RAISE program, but many have been rescinded by the Trump administration.

Taking on Ultra-Processed Foods & Obesity

THE HHS IS phasing out petroleum-based food dyes and struck a deal with drugmakers to bring down the cost of blockbuster obesity medications such as Ozempic. The administration has also added more than $60 million to help improve the nutrition of children enrolled in Head Start. At the same time, millions of low-income Americans face new restrictions on food assistance.

Yes and no. If you need a drug like Ozempic, the cheaper price could be a game changer. Working with the industry to transition to natural dyes from petroleum-based ones is a small, healthy step—although it overlooks the major concerns with ultra-processed foods, including all the calories, sodium, fat, and refined sugar they come with.

“RFK Jr. is absolutely right that America is not doing well, but here’s where he goes wrong: He’s taking legitimate concerns, and he often boils them down to single villains and single-bullet solutions,” says Dr. Adams. “Silver-bullet solutions aren’t going to make people or communities healthier again, unless we take a step back and look at the broader societal causes of these issues.”

Recent moves will likely make it harder for millions of Americans to eat nutritious foods, which are generally more expensive than processed ones. Congress recently enacted the largest budget cuts in history to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, which helps feed more than 40 million Americans, and eliminated SNAP Education, which helps families stretch budgets and cook healthy meals.

Investigating the Causes of Autism

THE ADMINISTRATION IMPLICATED acetaminophen, the most widely taken drug during pregnancy, as a cause of autism and is promoting leucovorin as a treatment. In November, a CDC website that previously explained that vaccines don’t cause autism now promotes a long-debunked connection between the shots and the condition.

No. The acetaminophen announcement simplifies autism—which is likely driven by a complex interaction between genetics and environment—in a way that isn’t actually helpful and recalls a painful era of mom-blaming (as in Maybe your kid wouldn’t have this condition if you hadn’t taken that Tylenol).

Scientists and parents have been struggling for years to make sense of the rise in autism diagnoses, beyond a broader definition and wider screening. Kennedy entered office saying he was expecting to start providing answers by September 2025 (as if the scientists working for decades had just been too inept to get to the bottom of it). “I thought that was an ambitious goal, and if they were going to put all their resources together, that’d be great,” says Robert L. Hendren, DO, of the Center for Autism Spectrum Disorders at the University of California, San Francisco.

Even after the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists affirmed that “evidence does not support a causal link between prenatal acetaminophen use and neurodevelopmental disorders,” the announcement created fear, doubt, and alarm. As for leucovorin as a treatment: “It may be relevant for some people at some period of time,” Dr. Hendren says, but it’s not a panacea.

And about the vaccine theory—no environmental factor has been studied more. Numerous rigorous studies conducted around the world have failed to find a link.

Why don’t we know what causes autism? “It’s at least 100 genes, and they’re not the same genes for each person,” Dr. Hendren says. Those genes interact with something not yet understood, or with many things, in a child’s environment to affect the brain. That’s why autism symptoms fall on a spectrum. “There’s this old saying,” Dr. Hendren says. “You see one kid with autism, you’ve seen one kid with autism.”

Reducing Fluoride Exposure

IN APRIL, THE HHS secretary called for states to ban added fluoride from drinking water. (His remarks came during a news conference in Utah celebrating the state’s prohibition on water fluoridation.) The FDA has also restricted the sale of unapproved prescription ingestible fluoride products.

Unlikely. The mineral has been added to public water supplies since the 1940s, when dental health was so bad that many WWII recruits were turned away because they didn’t have six top teeth that met six bottom ones. Cavities cause more than just pain: They can lead to missed work, poor diet, and tooth loss, and they cost money to fix. Fluoride in drinking water drops rates of tooth decay by 25 percent in cities that have it compared with cities that don’t.

Opponents of fluoridated water are concerned about research that suggests a correlation between high levels of fluoride exposure and reduced cognitive function in adults as well as lower IQ scores in kids. (The results include data from countries where people are exposed to high fluoride levels not only from water but also from sources like burning coal, which produces a stew of pollutants linked to impaired cognitive development.)

Proponents point to science showing that the link appears only with fluoridation levels at least twice as high as the legal limit in water supplies. At the current levels of U.S. water fluoridation, “there’s no evidence of concern with IQ,” says Steven M. Levy, DDS, a professor at the University of Iowa College of Dentistry and Dental Clinics.

If you drink tap water and fluoride is removed from your local supply, you’ll have to be really diligent about tooth brushing, using an oral rinse, and getting dental care. Not everyone has access to that, and those are the people who need fluoride most. Juneau, Alaska, is a case in point. The city stopped fluoridating its water almost 20 years ago. Back then, kids on Medicaid had about 1.5 cavities a year. After the fluoride was gone, that number rose to about 2.5.

4 Ways to Protect Your Health—That Have Nothing to Do With Politics

Tyler Evans, MD, outlines what to do now.

RIGHT NOW, WE are experiencing a new kind of uncertainty when it comes to American health. Policies are shifting fast, often with more attention to political symbolism than evidence, and the result is a landscape where the guardrails that once quietly protected people are becoming thinner. When you spend enough years practicing medicine in places where health systems have collapsed, you learn a difficult truth: People suffer when the structures around them fail to protect them. I have worked in places where public health was stripped to the bone, where illness surged not because people made bad decisions but because the systems around them disappeared. When I look at what is happening in the U.S., I think about those experiences.

Public health is designed to make healthy choices easy. It sets standards, funds prevention programs, expands access to early detection, supports mental health resources, and ensures that evidence shapes national guidance. When those structures weaken, risk finds room to grow in the gaps that appear. Men feel the effects first in higher blood pressure, in missed cancer screenings, in delayed vaccinations, and in unaddressed stress. The shift is gradual, but the consequences accumulate.

The reality we’re in is that vaccines are becoming a personal puzzle rather than a national benchmark. Cancer research has lost investments that once turned scientific discovery into survival. Even debates about nutrition have drifted toward sentimentality. The idea that beef tallow or cane sugar alone will protect us from chronic illness reflects a desire for simplicity in a world that is far more complex. The same is true for fluoride, which has become a flashpoint with the push to reduce fluoride exposure. MAHA has given Americans more autonomy while removing the infrastructure that once made that autonomy safe.

To be fair, there are a few encouraging signals. Americans are paying more attention to movement and have started questioning the role of ultra-processed foods. But awareness without support simply assigns individuals more responsibility while leaving the systemic barriers unchanged.

So the real question is: How can men protect themselves in a moment when national prevention efforts are fading? The strongest step men can take is to act early, act consistently, and act with the understanding that personal choices matter more when national systems are stepping back. Not because the burden should fall entirely on individuals, but because health does not pause while politics reorganizes itself. Here’s where to start.

1. Stay current on vaccines, even if the messaging feels muddled.

Vaccines remain one of the most effective tools in medicine. They prevent hospitalizations, reduce long-term complications, and protect the people around you. If national campaigns are quieter, you still have local options. Pharmacies, clinics, and community health workers can guide you.

2. Get screened before you feel sick.

In my clinical work, the most heartbreaking cases are the ones where a disease had been quietly advancing for years. Early detection matters for cancer, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic issues. An annual checkup is not a luxury. It is a lifeline.

3. Move in ways that reduce stress.

Men are often told to chase peak performance. That is not what health requires. What protects you is steady movement. Walking. Strength training. Any activity that lowers inflammation and supports mental resilience. It is not about chasing an ideal. It is about consistency.

4. Protect your mental health by building real connections.

Loneliness weakens the body as much as the mind. Men often believe they should bear stress alone. That belief is killing us. Find someone you can talk to. Reach out to people who matter. Connection is medicine, and it is in short supply right now.
