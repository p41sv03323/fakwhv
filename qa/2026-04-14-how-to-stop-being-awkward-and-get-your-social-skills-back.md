# How to Stop Being Awkward and Get Your Social Skills Back

Just when you got over Zoom awkwardness, IRL awkwardness elbowed its way back into your life. There you are getting out of your car at the dry cleaner or standing at the dog park or grocery store with someone you kinda know and…what are you supposed to say again? I just got back from my first psychology conference in a long time, and while it felt great to finally be around people, my social-skills A game was AWOL. Just making small talk felt like so much work.

I was reminded of the sessions I’ve had with patients who have a lot of anxiety around casual conversations: people concerned with saying the right thing at the right time or worried about what the person they’re talking to thinks about what they’re saying. Here’s the truth about being an adept conversationalist: What you say isn’t as important as the fact that you listen. Listening well creates a comfortable and supportive environment that can keep a good conversation going or even turn it into a great one.

During psychiatry training, I was taught that active listening means making decent eye contact, paying attention to details, and doing my best to avoid thinking about what’s for lunch. At home, it means putting down the phone and doing all of the above. Active listening isn’t easy. In fact, it’s freaking hard (trust me; I do it for a living), because we have a tendency to want to get our point across, interrupt, or zone out. But a shorthand strategy I use is: “Let the other person do 80 to 90 percent of the talking.” To become a first-rate listener, take three days this week and try one of the following challenges each day. Implement the ones that worked best (usually the hardest ones) when you feel conversational anxiety creeping in. Turning these skills into habits can take years, but you’ll enjoy the benefits of deeper relationships and less anxiety along the way.

Day 1: Listen, Then Ask

The challenge: Intentionally ask at least one question—without interrupting—in every social conversation today.

What it does for you: Focusing on what you’re going to say next makes it harder to remember what someone just told you. Instead of planning how you’re going to respond, try asking a question based on something you just heard. It could be as simple as “That sounds interesting—can you tell me more about it?” If the other person talks about a difficult experience, like a loss in the family, “I’m sorry to hear that” isn’t a bad thing to say, since it offers empathy. But it’s good to follow up with a question such as “What were they like?” People would rather reflect fondly on a loved one’s life than dwell on their death. Don’t worry about any awkwardness. They’re sharing information with you because they’re at ease around you. A question helps someone know you’re actively listening, and it fosters an environment where they’ll probably want to share more with you. As a bonus, they’re more likely to ask you questions, too, which can spark a stimulating conversation.

Day 2: Put Your Agenda Aside

[and Get Your](https://innate-signal.jcesqc8.com/)

The challenge: Before you jump into a conversation to make a point, ask yourself if it will help you understand whom you’re talking to better.

What it does for you: I get the need to make a good point, particularly if you’re opinionated like I am. But using a little restraint and sidelining your agenda can allow you to hear things you may otherwise miss. Discussing what’s on your mind—especially if no one asked—is one of the surest ways to wreck your listening skills, since it quickly shifts the conversation back to you. While you might walk away feeling like you had a good talk, in the end, you don’t know anything about the other person because you were so focused on explaining your position. I’m not saying that if you have something to say you should sit like a rock and keep your mouth shut. But if you’re about to interject just to get someone to understand you, maybe hold off until they’re finished making their argument, or until you’re directly asked about yours.

Day 3: Turn Down Your Emo Volume

[Get Your](https://collagen-atelier.bbsxyxy.com/)

The challenge: Every time someone says something that riles you up or makes you tune out, breathe in for four seconds, hold for seven, then breathe out for eight.

What it does for you: When whatever this person said touches an emotional nerve, it can seem impossible to hear anything else that follows. For instance, nobody hears a word after “We need to lay you off.” Although turning down your emotional volume can feel like trying to put out a five-alarm fire, it really is possible to extinguish the flames. Your mind is raging all over the place when you’re scared, pissed off, or even detached in a conversation, so you need to learn how to ground yourself in the moment. This allows you to be present and process what’s being said. To do this, I recommend the breathing pattern above (4-7-8 breathing). Biologically, this boosts the neurotransmitter GABA, which decreases activity in your nervous system and activates the calming part of that system. Emotionally, it brings you out of your own head, sends you back to earth, and makes you ready to listen. Don’t wait until you’re triggered by something to try this. Today, practice a few rounds of 4-7-8 breathing so that when you have to use it, you’ll be prepared.

Have some fun with this challenge and you might find yourself looking for even more opportunities to be in situations where you’ll not only need your social skills but also want to enjoy the deeper conversations they foster.
